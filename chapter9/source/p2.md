
## 9.4 Dynamic Voltage Restorer (DVR)

A DVR is a series-connected VSC that inserts a controlled voltage between the supply and a sensitive load, so that the load voltage stays at its reference while the supply voltage sags, swells, becomes unbalanced or distorted. The first utility DVR entered service in 1996 on the Duke Power system at Anderson, South Carolina (12.47 kV), and the device has since become a common way to protect a large sensitive load, such as a semiconductor fab or a paper machine, against sags caused by faults elsewhere on the network [20]. The series connection is what makes it economical: a DVR supplies only the missing voltage, so its rating is a fraction of the load rating for sags of practical depth.

### 9.4.1 Components and operating principle

FIG{dvr} shows a complete DVR. Its main parts are:

- a voltage-source converter, usually a three-leg or three H-bridge IGBT inverter switched at several kHz;
- a DC link and energy source, which supplies the real power the injection requires (Section 9.4.2);
- an injection (series) transformer in each phase, which isolates the converter from the line, matches its voltage and current to the line, and blocks zero sequence where required;
- a passive filter, normally an $L_f$-$C_f$ low-pass filter on the converter side of the transformer (line-side filters avoid transformer harmonic heating but expose the filter to fault current);
- a bypass switch (antiparallel thyristors or a mechanical switch) that short-circuits the transformer's line winding during downstream faults, overloads or maintenance; and
- a controller with voltage and current sensors, a PLL and disturbance detection.

![FIGDEF{dvr} Complete DVR arrangement: injection transformer, LC filter, VSC, DC link or energy storage, bypass switch and controller.](fig/image20.png){width=86%}

With transformer and filter quantities referred to the line side, the governing ideal phasor relationship is

$$\mathbf{V}_L=\mathbf{V}_s+\mathbf{V}_{inj}$$ EQDEF{dvrkvl}

where $\mathbf{V}_L$, $\mathbf{V}_s$ and $\mathbf{V}_{inj}$ are RMS voltage phasors at the same terminals and positive injection is a rise from source to load, the voltage-rise convention of Chapter 8. Sag depth is $X=1-|V_s|/V_{Lc}$, where $V_{Lc}$ is the rated (target) load voltage. The real and reactive power the DVR exchanges with the line are, per phase,

$$P_{DVR}+jQ_{DVR}=\mathbf{V}_{inj}\mathbf{I}_{DVR}^{*}$$ EQDEF{sdvr}

and for a standalone DVR the series current equals the load current, $\mathbf{I}_{DVR}=\mathbf{I}_L$.

A DVR has three operating modes. In standby the supply is healthy and the DVR injects nothing; either the converter applies zero voltage (shorting the transformer's converter winding through its switches) or the bypass switch carries the load current, which keeps losses low. In boost (injection) mode a disturbance is detected and the DVR injects the missing voltage within a few milliseconds. In protection mode a downstream fault drives current far above rating, so the bypass switch closes and the converter is blocked.

### 9.4.2 Classification and energy-source topologies

DVRs are classified, like the DSTATCOM, by converter type (CSC or VSC), by supply system (single-phase, three-phase three-wire and three-phase four-wire) and by bridge topology. A single-phase DVR may use a half bridge or a full bridge (FIG{dvr1ph}). For the same DC voltage the full bridge produces twice the AC output voltage; the half bridge needs two series capacitors and a doubled DC voltage, but only two switches. A three-phase DVR uses either a three-leg VSC feeding three injection transformers with isolated windings (FIG{dvr3ph}) or three H-bridges. Three H-bridges, or a four-leg converter, allow independent phase voltages including zero sequence, which a three-leg bridge with delta-connected windings cannot produce. Multilevel and cascaded structures reduce device voltage stress and $dv/dt$ at higher distribution voltages but need more devices, isolated supplies or capacitor balancing. Transformerless DVRs remove the injection transformer but transfer isolation, common-mode voltage and fault-withstand requirements to the converter and its insulation.

![FIGDEF{dvr1ph} Single-phase DVR: (a) half-bridge VSC, (b) full-bridge VSC.](fig/image21.png){width=86%}

![FIGDEF{dvr3ph} Three-phase three-wire DVR: three injection transformers driven by a three-leg VSC with ripple filters.](fig/image22.png){width=74%}

The most important classification for design is by the source of the injected energy (FIG{dvr_energy}) [12]:

- *No storage, supply-side (left-shunt) rectifier.* A rectifier fed from the supply side charges the DC link. The DVR can ride through sags of any duration, but the rectifier's input voltage falls with the sag, so the available DC voltage and power fall exactly when they are needed. Deep sags cannot be corrected unless the DC link is oversized.
- *No storage, load-side (right-shunt) rectifier.* The rectifier is fed from the restored load voltage, so its input stays at rated value. The extra rectifier current flows through the injection transformer, and the scheme forms a positive-feedback loop that must be controlled carefully.
- *Stored energy (BESS, supercapacitor, flywheel).* A battery or other store, usually through a DC/DC converter, supplies real power. The DVR can use in-phase injection (minimum voltage rating) and can also preserve phase, but the store must be sized for the worst sag depth and duration (Section 9.4.5).
- *Capacitor-supported (self-supported).* Only a DC capacitor is present. Ideally the DVR then exchanges no average real power with the line, so it must inject in quadrature with the load current. As shown in Section 9.4.3, this restores a sag only if the sag is shallow enough for the load power factor, and a small real-power exchange is still needed to cover losses [18].

![FIGDEF{dvr_energy} DVR energy-source options: (a) supply-side rectifier, (b) load-side rectifier, (c) battery (BESS) with DC/DC converter, (d) capacitor-supported.](fig/image23.png){width=62%}

Nielsen and Blaabjerg's comparison [12] concluded that, for the same power rating, a supply-side rectifier scheme without storage is simple and cheap and handles long shallow sags well, while a stored-energy DVR is the better choice when deep sags must be corrected and the stored energy needed is modest. Two DVRs with the same series-converter kVA can therefore have very different ride-through durations (TAB{dvr_energy}).

Table: TABDEF{dvr_energy} DVR energy-source and topology choices.

| Choice | Advantage | Limitation that must be checked |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Capacitor-supported | Simple DC side | Short real-power duration and DC-voltage droop |
| BESS-supported | Longer real-power support | Battery power capability, state of charge and converter losses |
| Supercapacitor-supported | High pulse power and cycle life | Lower energy density and wide voltage variation |
| Supply-side rectifier | No dedicated storage for moderate sags | Source must retain enough voltage and power |
| Multilevel VSC | Lower device stress and better waveform | Balancing, device count and protection complexity |
| Transformerless DVR | Reduced magnetic hardware | Isolation, common-mode and fault-duty challenges |

### 9.4.3 Compensation strategies

The DVR can reach the target load-voltage magnitude with many different injections, because only $|V_L|$ is specified. FIG{dvr_strat} shows the strategies in common use for a sag that also carries a phase jump.

![FIGDEF{dvr_strat} DVR compensation strategies for a sag with a phase jump: in-phase, pre-sag and phase-advance injection (the quadrature strategy is treated separately below).](fig/image24.png){width=62%}

*In-phase injection.* The injected voltage is in phase with the disturbed supply voltage. With $\mathbf{V}_s$ as reference, the injection is a signed scalar,

$$V_{inj}=V_{Lc}-|V_s|=V_{Lc}X$$ EQDEF{inphase}

which is the minimum injection magnitude of any strategy, so it gives the smallest voltage rating and the smallest transformer. For a standalone DVR carrying $I_L$ at load power-factor angle $\phi$,

$$P_{DVR}=\left(V_{Lc}-|V_s|\right)I_L\cos\phi,\qquad Q_{DVR}=\left(V_{Lc}-|V_s|\right)I_L\sin\phi$$ EQDEF{pinphase}

For a balanced three-phase sag this means $P_{DVR}=XP_L$: the DVR supplies exactly the fraction of load power that the source can no longer deliver. During a swell ($X<0$) the signs reverse and the DVR absorbs real power, which needs a regenerative path, a store that can accept charge or a dissipative chopper. In-phase injection does not preserve the pre-sag phase.

*Pre-sag compensation.* Phase-sensitive loads, such as line-commutated converters and some drives, are disturbed by a phase jump as well as by a magnitude drop. The DVR then restores the pre-sag phasor itself,

$$\mathbf{V}_{inj}=\mathbf{V}_{L,pre}-\mathbf{V}_s$$ EQDEF{presag}

which needs a larger injection, and usually more real energy, than in-phase compensation. The PLL must hold the pre-disturbance angle during the event [17].

*Phase-advance (energy-optimized) compensation.* If the load tolerates a phase shift, the load voltage can be rotated ahead of the supply so that the injection contains a larger reactive part and less real power, extending ride-through for a given store [16]. The price is a larger injection voltage and a phase transition at the start and end of the event.

*Quadrature (zero-real-power) compensation.* If the injection is perpendicular to the series current, $P_{DVR}=\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{V}_{inj}\mathbf{I}_{DVR}^{*}\}=0$ and, ideally, no stored energy is needed. This is the only steady-state option for a capacitor-supported DVR. The current reference is decisive: a standalone DVR carries the load current, so its injection must be perpendicular to $\mathbf{I}_L$, not to $\mathbf{V}_s$. In the right-shunt UPQC of Section 9.5 the series current is the source current, which is in phase with $\mathbf{V}_s$; that is a different geometry.

#### Exact feasibility of zero-real-power restoration

Choose $\mathbf{V}_s$ real and let $\mathbf{V}_L=V_{Lc}e^{j\theta_L}$ and $\mathbf{I}_L=|I_L|e^{j(\theta_L-\phi)}$. Expanding $\mathrm{Re}\{(\mathbf{V}_L-\mathbf{V}_s)\mathbf{I}_L^{*}\}=0$ gives

$$\cos\left(\theta_L-\phi\right)=\frac{V_{Lc}\cos\phi}{|V_s|}$$ EQDEF{zpcond}

A real solution requires the right-hand side not to exceed one, so a standalone DVR can restore a sag without real power only if

$$|V_s|\ge V_{Lc}\cos\phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X\le X_{max}=1-\cos\phi$$ EQDEF{xmax}

Physically, the source must still deliver the full load real power $V_{Lc}I_L\cos\phi$ through the same current magnitude $I_L$, and it can deliver at most $|V_s||I_L|$. FIG{xmax} plots this limit. A load at 0.85 power factor can have a 15 % sag corrected by a capacitor-supported DVR, and a unity-power-factor load cannot have any sag corrected without real power. At the limit, $\theta_L=\phi$, the load current comes into phase with $\mathbf{V}_s$, the injection is perpendicular to $\mathbf{V}_s$ and its magnitude is $V_{Lc}\sin\phi$. A formula giving the limit as $1-\sin\phi$ appears in some teaching material; it confuses the limiting injection $V_{Lc}\sin\phi$ with the limiting supply voltage and must not be used.

![FIGDEF{xmax} Exact zero-real-power feasibility limit of a standalone DVR versus load power factor.](fig/image25.png){width=72%}

Where the sag is feasible, both roots must be checked:

$$\theta_L=\phi\pm\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{V_{Lc}\cos\phi}{|V_s|}\right),\qquad \mathbf{V}_{inj}=V_{Lc}e^{j\theta_L}-|V_s|$$ EQDEF{roots}

For a 230 V, 0.8 lagging-pf load with $|V_s|=207$ V the roots are $9.604^\circ$ and $64.136^\circ$, with injections of $43.17\angle62.73^\circ$ V and $232.83\angle117.27^\circ$ V (FIG{roots}). Both give zero real power; the smaller one is preferred if its load-phase shift is acceptable.

![FIGDEF{roots} The two zero-real-power roots for a 230 V, 0.8 pf load and a 207 V supply.](fig/image26.png){width=62%}

*General-angle injection.* For a specified injection magnitude $U=|V_{inj}|$, the law of cosines on the triangle of EQ{dvrkvl} gives the injection angle measured from $\mathbf{V}_s$:

$$\cos\psi=\frac{V_{Lc}^{2}-|V_s|^{2}-U^{2}}{2|V_s|U}$$ EQDEF{psi}

Both signs of $\psi$ are geometrically admissible when $\lvert V_{Lc}-\lvert V_s\rvert\rvert\le U\le V_{Lc}+\lvert V_s\rvert$. In-phase injection is the minimum-$U$ boundary. Choosing the angle is a feasibility calculation, not an optimization by itself; an optimization needs an objective such as minimum energy, minimum converter VA or limited phase shift.

#### Unbalanced sags

A single-line-to-ground or line-to-line fault elsewhere sags the three phases unequally, introduces negative-sequence voltage and can add a phase jump. Restoring each phase magnitude independently, with the fault's phase relationships still in place, would not reconstruct a balanced set. Using the symmetrical components of Chapter 2 with $V_0=0$ (an assumption that must be checked, because a grounded-wye winding can support zero sequence),

$$V_a=V_p+V_n,\qquad V_b=a^{2}V_p+aV_n,\qquad V_c=aV_p+a^{2}V_n$$ EQDEF{seq}

and the required injections for the balanced target $V_{Lc}\angle0^\circ$, $V_{Lc}\angle-120^\circ$, $V_{Lc}\angle120^\circ$ are

$$V_{inj,k}=V_{k,target}-V_{s,k},\qquad k=a,b,c$$ EQDEF{injk}

If $V_0\neq0$ it must also be subtracted, and the DVR's bridge, transformer connection and neutral path must be able to synthesize zero sequence. FIG{unbal} shows the case $V_p=0.85$ pu, $V_n=0.15$ pu, worked in Example EX{ex_unbal}. Unbalanced injection carries a second-harmonic real-power pulsation (visible in FIG{sim_dvr_unbal}), which adds DC-link ripple even when the average real power is modest.

![FIGDEF{unbal} Sequence-defined unbalanced sag ($V_p=0.85$ pu, $V_n=0.15$ pu) and the per-phase injections that restore the balanced target.](fig/image27.png){width=52%}

#### Voltage harmonics and notches

A DVR with enough bandwidth can also inject the negative of supply-voltage harmonics, $v_{inj,h}=-v_{s,h}$, and fill in commutation notches, so the load sees a clean sinusoid. This is series active filtering (Section 9.6). The injection-transformer leakage inductance and the $L_f$-$C_f$ filter limit the achievable bandwidth; selective harmonic controllers are used at medium voltage to keep the switching frequency low.

### 9.4.4 Control of the DVR

FIG{dvrctrl} shows the usual SRF-based controller. The supply voltages are transformed to the dq frame with the angle from a PLL. For pre-sag compensation the PLL is frozen at the pre-disturbance angle when a sag is detected; for in-phase compensation it continues to track the supply. The load-voltage reference in the same frame is $v_{Ld}^{*}=V_m$ (rated peak phase voltage) and $v_{Lq}^{*}=0$, so the feedforward injection reference is

$$v_{inj,d}^{*}=V_m-v_{sd},\qquad v_{inj,q}^{*}=-v_{sq}$$ EQDEF{dvrref}

Transforming back gives $v_{inj,abc}^{*}=v_L^{*}-v_s$, which corrects magnitude, phase, unbalance (if negative sequence is handled, for example by a second frame rotating at $-\omega$) and harmonics in one step. A feedback loop on the measured load voltage removes the drops across the filter and transformer leakage. The injected voltage is produced by a PWM voltage controller, often with an inner loop on filter-capacitor current for active damping of the $L_f$-$C_f$ resonance.

For a capacitor-supported DVR, a DC-voltage PI adds a small real-power component so that the converter absorbs its own losses:

$$v_{loss}(n)=v_{loss}(n-1)+K_{p1}\left[v_{de}(n)-v_{de}(n-1)\right]+K_{i1}v_{de}(n),\qquad v_{de}=v_{dc}^{*}-v_{dc}$$ EQDEF{vloss}

and a second PI on the load-voltage amplitude error sets the quadrature component.

![FIGDEF{dvrctrl} SRF-based DVR control with feedforward, load-voltage feedback and (for capacitor-supported DVRs) a DC-voltage loop.](fig/image28.png){width=92%}

Disturbance detection decides how fast the DVR acts. The useful response time is the sum of detection, reference calculation, PWM delay and the build-up of the output filter, and the load sees the uncompensated disturbance throughout. Half-cycle or full-cycle RMS methods are robust to noise but cannot detect a sag in a few milliseconds; peak detection, the dq-frame magnitude $\sqrt{v_d^2+v_q^2}$ and missing-voltage methods (comparing the instantaneous voltage with its pre-sag waveform) respond in 1 to 2 ms. A design should therefore state both the restored steady-state voltage and the maximum transient deviation during detection and injection build-up. The controller must also apply converter voltage and current limits, DC-energy limits, injection-transformer flux limits, anti-windup, controlled recovery ramps and bypass logic. A step change of injected voltage at the wrong instant of the cycle can saturate the injection transformer, because flux is the time integral of voltage; flux estimation, soft insertion or a command that limits the first-cycle volt-seconds prevents this.

### 9.4.5 Energy storage and DC-link requirements

For a storage-supported standalone DVR, injected positive real power and converter losses deplete the store. For arbitrary phase injections the average converter AC real power is $P_{DVR}=\sum_k\mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf{V}_{inj,k}\mathbf{I}_k^{*}\}$; for balanced operation it is $3|V_{inj}||I|\cos\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is the angle between injection and current, and in an in-phase standalone DVR $\gamma=\phi$. For positive, approximately constant delivered power and a stated conversion efficiency $\eta$,

$$E=\frac{P_{DVR}}{\eta}\,t_{sag}$$ EQDEF{energy}

The general balance, which also covers zero and negative real power, is

$$E_{drawn}=\int_0^{t}\left[P_{DVR}(\tau)+P_{loss}(\tau)\right]d\tau$$ EQDEF{energyint}

Pure quadrature operation has $P_{DVR}=0$ but still needs $\int P_{loss}\,dt$. During absorbing operation the signed power charges the store, and charging limits must be checked. For a capacitor allowed to fall from $V_{dc,max}$ to $V_{dc,min}$, the usable energy is $\frac{1}{2}C(V_{dc,max}^2-V_{dc,min}^2)$, so the minimum capacitance is

$$C_{dc}=\frac{2E}{V_{dc,max}^{2}-V_{dc,min}^{2}}$$ EQDEF{cdvr}

For an in-phase standalone DVR at fixed load power, $P_{DVR}=XP_L$, so $C_{dc}=2XP_Lt/[\eta(V_{dc,max}^2-V_{dc,min}^2)]$ is linear in both depth and duration (FIG{dvr_e}). Long events quickly make a pure capacitor impractical, which is why batteries, supercapacitors, flywheels or rectifier-supported links are used for longer ride-through.

![FIGDEF{dvr_e} Left: DC energy versus sag duration at 6 kW AC real power. Right: energy-based capacitance of an in-phase standalone DVR versus sag depth (20 kW load, 700 to 650 V droop).](fig/image29.png){width=86%}

Stored energy alone does not size a DVR. The store must provide the event energy, its interface must provide the event power, and the DC-link voltage must stay high enough for the converter to synthesize the commanded AC voltage. A battery with ample watt-hours may still be unsuitable if its allowable discharge current is too small, while a supercapacitor may supply the pulse power but need a wide DC/DC conversion range as its voltage falls. For repeated sags, recharge time and thermal accumulation must also be checked.

### 9.4.6 Design of a DVR

The design covers the voltage, current and VA ratings of the VSC, the injection transformer, the DC-bus voltage and capacitance, the interfacing inductance and the ripple filter [2].

*Voltage rating.* The injection voltage is set by the worst disturbance to be corrected and by the strategy. For in-phase injection on a balanced sag, $V_{inj}=XV_{Lc}$, plus an allowance for unbalance (for example 6 % negative sequence) and for phase jumps. Quadrature injection needs $\sqrt{V_{Lc}^2-|V_s|^2}$ in a UPQC and the larger standalone root in EQ{roots}, so it raises the voltage rating sharply.

*Current rating.* A standalone DVR carries the full load current, $I_{DVR}=I_L$, and its kVA rating is $S_{DVR}=3V_{inj}I_L$ for a three-phase unit.

*Injection transformer.* Its line winding carries $I_L$ continuously and must withstand downstream fault current until the bypass closes, so it has three distinct duties: continuous current, compensation voltage and short-duration fault withstand. Its rating equals the converter rating. The turns ratio puts the converter at a convenient voltage and current: $n=V_{VSC}/V_{inj}$, with converter-side current $I_L/n$. The core must not saturate when the injection starts at a voltage zero with full magnitude, so it is commonly designed for about twice the steady flux, or the controller limits the initial flux.

*DC-bus voltage.* For a three-leg VSC with sinusoidal PWM, $V_{dc}\ge2\sqrt2V_{VSC}/m$, where $V_{VSC}$ is the converter-side phase RMS voltage. For a full bridge, $V_{dc}\ge\sqrt2V_{VSC}/m$.

*DC-bus capacitance.* In a BESS- or rectifier-supported DVR the capacitor only filters ripple and covers the control delay, sized from $\frac{1}{2}C(V_{dc}^2-V_{dc1}^2)=3V_{inj}I_L\Delta t$ for a short support time $\Delta t$ of a few hundred microseconds. In a capacitor-supported DVR that injects real power, EQ{cdvr} with the full event duration applies.

*Interfacing inductance.* Referred through the transformer ratio $n$,

$$L_r=\frac{n\,(\sqrt3/2)\,mV_{dc}}{6\,a\,f_s\,\Delta i_s}$$ EQDEF{lrdvr}

where $\Delta i_s$ is the permitted line-side ripple. Without the factor $n$ this is EQ{lr}.

*Ripple filter.* As for the DSTATCOM, $R_r$ and $C_r$ are chosen with a corner frequency of about $f_s/2$.

### 9.4.7 Protection

The DVR sits in series with the load, so every downstream fault current passes through its transformer. Protection must close the bypass switch within a fraction of a cycle and block the converter before its switches exceed their current rating; a thyristor crowbar across the converter winding is a common backup. Upstream faults are, by contrast, the reason the DVR exists, and it must not bypass during them. The detection logic must therefore distinguish a supply-side sag (low voltage, normal or low current) from a load-side fault (high current). Coordination with upstream overcurrent relays matters because the DVR's injection and current limit change the fault current those relays see. Transformer inrush at energization, standby losses and the thermal capacity of the bypass for long outages complete the protection study.

### 9.4.9 The interline DVR (IDVR)

An IDVR shares a DC link between series compensators on different feeders (FIG{idvr}), following the interline power-balance principle of Chapter 8. When one feeder sags, the DVR on a healthy feeder absorbs real power from its own line (by injecting a small voltage component in phase opposition to that feeder's current), and the sagged feeder's DVR exports it. The healthy feeder's load voltage must remain acceptable while this happens, so the healthy feeder is not an unlimited source. The common link still needs capacitance for switching ripple, unbalanced power pulsation and transient mismatch.

### 9.4.10 Worked examples: DVR

#### EXDEF{ex_bess1}. Single-phase BESS-supported DVR with in-phase injection

A single-phase 5 kVA, 230 V, 50 Hz, 0.85 lagging pf critical load is protected by a BESS-supported DVR using in-phase injection. The supply sags by 25 %. Find the injected voltage, the DVR current, its kVA rating and the real power drawn from the battery.

**Solution.** $V_s=230\times0.75=172.5$ V and $V_{inj}=57.5$ V. $I_{DVR}=I_L=5000/230=21.74$ A, so $S_{DVR}=57.5\times21.74=1.25$ kVA. From EQ{pinphase}, $P_{DVR}=57.5\times21.74\times0.85=1063$ W and $Q_{DVR}=658$ var.

#### EXDEF{ex_upf_fail}. Capacitor-supported DVR with a unity-power-factor load

A single-phase capacitor-supported DVR is proposed for a 10 kVA, 230 V, unity-pf load facing a 30 % sag, using an injection of $\sqrt{230^2-161^2}=164.25$ V at right angles to the supply voltage. Check whether this injection really needs no real power.

**Solution.** With $V_s=161$ V real and $V_{inj}=j164.25$ V, the load voltage is $230\angle45.57^\circ$ V. A unity-pf load draws its current in phase with its own voltage, $I_L=43.48\angle45.57^\circ$ A. Then $P_{DVR}=\mathrm{Re}\{V_{inj}I_L^{*}\}=164.25\times43.48\times\cos(90^\circ-45.57^\circ)=5.10$ kW, about half the load power. By EQ{xmax}, $X_{max}=1-\cos\phi=0$ for a unity-pf load, so no sag can be corrected without real power, whatever the injection angle. The injection was perpendicular to $V_s$, but a standalone DVR's zero-power condition is perpendicularity to $I_L$. A capacitor-supported DVR would discharge within a fraction of a cycle.

#### EXDEF{ex_xmax}. Maximum sag for a capacitor-supported DVR

A capacitor-supported DVR protects a 415 V (239.6 V phase) load at 0.85 lagging pf. Find the maximum sag it can correct without real power, the injection at that limit and the phase shift of the load voltage.

**Solution.** From EQ{xmax}, $X_{max}=1-0.85=15$ %, so $|V_s|_{min}=203.7$ V. At the limit $\theta_L=\phi=31.79^\circ$, the injection is perpendicular to $V_s$ and $|V_{inj}|=239.6\sin31.79^\circ=126.2$ V. The load voltage leads the supply by $31.79^\circ$. The value 0.5268 pu is $\sin\phi$, the injection magnitude in per unit; mistaking it for the minimum supply voltage gives an incorrect "47 % maximum sag".

#### EXDEF{ex_schemes}. DVR rating for different injection schemes

A three-phase 415 V, 30 kVA, 0.8 pf critical load experiences a balanced 35 % sag. The phase current is 41.74 A. Compare in-phase injection with zero-real-power injection.

**Solution.** $|V_s|=0.65\times239.6=155.74$ V. In-phase: $V_{inj}=83.86$ V and $S_{DVR}=3\times83.86\times41.74=10.50$ kVA (35 % of the load rating), with $P_{DVR}=0.35\times24=8.4$ kW. Zero real power: by EQ{xmax} a 0.8 pf load allows at most a 20 % sag, so a 35 % sag is infeasible for a standalone capacitor-supported DVR. The figure of 182.1 V (22.8 kVA) obtained from $\sqrt{V_{Lc}^2-|V_s|^2}$ is the injection perpendicular to the supply voltage. It is the correct UPQC-Q series voltage (Section 9.5), where the series current is in phase with $V_s$, but it is not a zero-power solution for a standalone DVR carrying a lagging load current.

#### EXDEF{ex_swell}. Swell compensation and power direction

A single-phase DVR protects a 2.5 kVA, 230 V, unity-pf load against a 20 % swell. Find the injection, the kVA rating and the power direction.

**Solution.** $V_s=276$ V, so $V_{inj}=230-276=-46$ V (in phase opposition). $I_L=10.87$ A and $S_{DVR}=500$ VA. $P_{DVR}=-46\times10.87=-500$ W: the DVR absorbs 500 W, which must charge the store or be dissipated in a chopper.

#### EXDEF{ex_bess_e}. BESS energy for an extended sag

A three-phase 415 V, 15 kVA, 0.8 pf critical load is protected by a BESS-supported in-phase DVR against a 30 % sag lasting 500 ms. Find the load power, the battery power and the energy per event.

**Solution.** $P_L=12$ kW. $V_{inj}=71.88$ V and $I=20.86$ A, so $P_{BESS}=3\times71.88\times20.86\times0.8=3.60$ kW, which is $0.3P_L$. $E=3.60\times0.5=1.80$ kJ, or 0.50 Wh. At 95 % conversion efficiency the battery delivers 1.89 kJ. The energy is tiny, but the battery must deliver 3.6 kW for half a second: DVR storage is sized by power and discharge rate at least as much as by energy, which is why supercapacitors are attractive.

#### EXDEF{ex_store}. Standalone DVR energy storage

A balanced three-phase storage-supported DVR in in-phase mode protects a 415 V, 20 kW, 0.9 lagging pf load against a 30 % sag for 200 ms. Find the phase voltage, series current, injected and sagged phase voltages, delivered real power and ideal energy.

Ideal energy: $E=6000\times0.2=1200$ J. Source real power is 14 kW and DVR export 6 kW, totalling the 20 kW load demand.

#### EXDEF{ex_cap_loss}. Loss-inclusive capacitance

For Example EX{ex_store}, with the DC bus permitted to droop from 700 V to 650 V and $\eta=0.96$, find the DC-link capacitance and compare with the ideal value.

**Solution.** $E=(6000/0.96)\times0.2=1250$ J, 4.2 % above 1200 J. From EQ{cdvr}, $C_{dc}=2\times1250/(700^2-650^2)=37.04$ mF (ideal 35.56 mF). ## 9.5 Unified Power Quality Conditioner (UPQC)

A UPQC combines a series converter (the DVR part) and a shunt converter (the DSTATCOM part) on a common DC link [4], [23]. It is best read as a coordinated two-port power-quality controller rather than a DVR and a DSTATCOM placed in one cabinet. The series converter keeps the load voltage clean and regulated whatever the supply does; the shunt converter keeps the source current sinusoidal, balanced and in phase whatever the load draws, and it also regulates the shared DC voltage. The common DC link couples their real-power duties, and that coupling determines rating, control priority and disturbance capability. The UPQC is to distribution what the UPFC of Chapter 8 is to transmission: the same back-to-back structure with different objectives.

### 9.5.1 Structure and power flow

The series VSC is voltage-controlled through injection transformers; the shunt VSC is current-controlled through interfacing inductors. Both need ripple filters, and both share the DC capacitor. With $P_{into,k}$ defined as the real power absorbed from the AC system by converter $k$ (positive into the converter), the DC-link energy obeys

$$\frac{dW_{dc}}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}C_{dc}V_{dc}^{2}\right)=\sum_kP_{into,k}-P_{loss}$$ EQDEF{upqcdc}

At constant average DC energy, $\sum P_{into,k}=P_{loss}$. Equivalently, with AC exports positive, $P_{se}+P_{sh}+P_{loss}=0$. When the series converter exports real power to boost a sagged supply, the shunt converter must draw the same power from the AC system, so in a feasible grid-fed steady state the real power circulates: source to shunt converter, through the DC link, to series converter and back into the line. This is why a UPQC can correct long sags without a large energy store, provided the sagged source can still supply the load power at a higher current. The capacitor only buffers transient mismatch and ripple; it is not a source of sustained real power.

### 9.5.2 Classification

UPQCs are classified by converter type (CSC or VSC), by supply system (single-phase two-wire, three-phase three-wire and three-phase four-wire), by the location of the shunt converter and by how the series converter shares real and reactive power (FIG{upqc_class}) [4]. Extensions include the interline UPQC (series and shunt converters on different feeders), the multi-converter UPQC and UPQCs with distributed generation or storage on the DC link.

![FIGDEF{upqc_class} Classification of UPQC by converter structure, connection topology, supply system and compensation objective.](fig/image44.png){width=86%}

*Right-shunt and left-shunt UPQC.* In the right-shunt UPQC (FIG{rs_ls}(a)) the shunt converter is on the load side of the series converter. The series converter then carries the source current, which the shunt converter has made sinusoidal and in phase with the supply, and the shunt converter sees the regulated load voltage; during a sag it draws the series converter's real power from the restored bus, so the source current rises as the source voltage falls. In the left-shunt UPQC (FIG{rs_ls}(b)) the shunt converter is on the supply side: the series converter carries the load current, with its harmonics and reactive component, and the shunt converter sees the disturbed supply voltage. The right-shunt arrangement is usually preferred because the shunt converter works at a clean voltage, its current harmonics do not flow through the series transformer and the series converter carries a well-defined sinusoidal current. The left-shunt arrangement is used when the shunt converter must also compensate other loads at the PCC. The two arrangements can deliver similar external objectives but do not have identical converter ratings, so comparisons must be made at the same load, sag, source power factor and control objective.

![FIGDEF{rs_ls} (a) Right-shunt UPQC, (b) left-shunt UPQC.](fig/image34.png){width=90%}

*Single-phase and three-phase UPQCs.* Balanced three-phase phasor sizing can use a per-phase equivalent, but switching and control implementations are not obtained simply by repeating a single leg. A single-phase full bridge needs a complete return path; a three-wire three-phase UPQC uses three-leg converters; a four-wire UPQC needs a rated neutral path for the shunt converter, such as a fourth leg or a split DC link (FIG{upqc4w}). The neutral leg must be rated from its current, since its terminal voltage can be small while its conduction loss is large. Single-phase units have twice-line-frequency DC-energy ripple, and unbalanced three-phase operation also produces ripple; both enter the capacitor sizing.

![FIGDEF{upqc4w} Three-phase four-wire right-shunt UPQC: three-leg series VSC (DVR part) and four-leg shunt VSC (DSTATCOM part) on a shared DC link.](fig/image35.png){width=84%}

*UPQC-P, UPQC-Q and UPQC-S.* These names describe operating strategies, not different hardware. They state how the series converter injects its voltage relative to the series current:

- UPQC-P: injection in phase with the supply voltage and the (unity-pf) source current, so the series converter handles only real power. Its injection voltage is the smallest possible, but the shunt converter must circulate that real power.
- UPQC-Q: injection in quadrature with the source current, so the series converter handles only reactive power. It needs a larger injection voltage, and it cannot correct swells, because adding a perpendicular voltage always increases the resultant magnitude.
- UPQC-S: injection at a chosen angle, so the series converter handles both real and reactive power. Khadkikar and Chandra [11] used this to let the series converter share the load reactive power during normal operation as well as during sags and swells; a minimum-VA angle can also be chosen.

The derivations below use the right-shunt topology, so the series current equals the source current, unless stated otherwise.

### 9.5.3 Phasor analysis and converter ratings

Take the sagged supply voltage $V_s'=V_{Lc}(1-X)$ as real reference. With unity source power factor and a lossless UPQC, the source supplies exactly the load real power $P_L=V_{Lc}I_L\cos\phi$:

$$I_s=\frac{P_L}{V_s'}=\frac{I_L\cos\phi}{1-X}$$ EQDEF{is}

The source current rises as the supply sags. For a balanced three-phase system use per-phase quantities, or $I_s=P_{L,3\phi}/(\sqrt3V_{s,LL}')$. FIG{upqc_phasor} shows the three operating modes for a 30 % sag.

![FIGDEF{upqc_phasor} Phasor diagrams of a right-shunt UPQC during a 30 % sag: (a) UPQC-Q, (b) UPQC-P, (c) UPQC-S.](fig/image36.png){width=90%}

*UPQC-Q.* The injection is perpendicular to $I_s$, hence to $V_s'$, so $V_{Lc}^2=|V_s^{\prime}|^2+U^2$:

$$U=|V_{inj}|=\sqrt{V_{Lc}^{2}-|V_s^{\prime}|^{2}},\qquad \delta=\cos^{-1}(1-X)$$ EQDEF{upqcq}

where $\delta$ is the angle by which the load voltage leads the supply. The series converter's duty is entirely reactive:

$$S_{se}=UI_s=V_{Lc}I_L\cos\phi\tan\delta,\qquad P_{se}=0$$ EQDEF{sseq}

Writing $V_s'$ in place of $V_{Lc}$ in the last form is a common slip that underestimates the rating by the factor $1-X$. The load current lags the rotated load voltage by $\phi$, so it lies at $\delta-\phi$ from $V_s'$, and the shunt converter supplies the difference $I_{sh}=I_L-I_s$:

$$I_{sh}=I_L\frac{\sqrt{(1-X)^{2}+\cos^{2}\phi-2\cos\phi\cos(\phi-\delta)(1-X)}}{1-X}$$ EQDEF{ishq}

with $S_{sh}=V_{Lc}I_{sh}$. When $X=1-\cos\phi$, $\delta=\phi$ and $I_{sh}=0$: the rotation of the load voltage has brought the load current into phase with the supply, so the series converter alone supplies all the load's reactive power. At other sag depths the shunt current is nonzero, and its reactive direction reverses across that point.

*UPQC-P.* The injection is in phase with $V_s'$, $V_{inj}=V_{Lc}X$ (signed, negative for a swell). The series duty is entirely real:

$$S_{se}=|P_{se}|=V_{Lc}|X|I_s=V_{Lc}|X|\frac{I_L\cos\phi}{1-X}$$ EQDEF{ssep}

The factor $1/(1-X)$ is easily forgotten but essential: the series converter carries the increased source current, not the load's active current. The load voltage stays in phase with the supply, so the shunt current has a reactive part (the load's own) and an active part that returns the series converter's real power:

$$|I_{sh}|=I_L\sqrt{\sin^{2}\phi+\cos^{2}\phi\left(\frac{X}{1-X}\right)^{2}}$$ EQDEF{ishp}

*UPQC-S.* For a specified injection magnitude $U$, the angle measured from $V_s'$ follows from EQ{psi}, and

$$P_{se}=UI_s\cos\psi,\qquad Q_{se}=UI_s\sin\psi,\qquad \delta=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{U\sin\psi}{V_{Lc}}\right)$$ EQDEF{upqcs}

The shunt current is again $I_{sh}=I_L\angle(\delta-\phi)-I_s$. A minimum-combined-VA design varies the restored load angle $\delta$ and minimizes

$$J(\delta)=I_s\lvert V_{Lc}e^{j\delta}-\lvert V_s'\rvert\rvert+V_{Lc}\lvert I_Le^{j(\delta-\phi)}-I_s\rvert$$ EQDEF{J}

subject to each converter's voltage, current and VA limits, DC-energy balance and the permissible phase shift. FIG{J} plots $J$ for a 20 % sag on a 230 V, 25 A load: at 0.8 pf the minimum is the quadrature point ($\delta=36.87^\circ$, 3450 VA), while at 0.95 pf it lies at $\delta=10.5^\circ$ with 3132 VA.

![FIGDEF{J} Combined operating VA of a UPQC versus restored load-voltage angle for a 20 % sag (230 V, 25 A).](fig/f9_J.png){width=70%}

*Operating duty and installed rating.* The numbers above are operating duties at one sag depth. The installed rating of each converter must cover its own maximum voltage and current over every specified condition, and those maxima do not occur at the same point. In particular, at nominal supply voltage the series converter is idle and the shunt converter supplies the whole load reactive current $I_L\sin\phi$. FIG{upqc_duty} shows the series, shunt and total duties versus sag depth for a 230 V, 25 A, 0.8 pf load: in UPQC-Q the shunt duty falls from 3450 VA at nominal voltage to zero at a 20 % sag and rises again, while the series duty grows steadily; in UPQC-P the series duty is small but the shunt duty never falls below the nominal 3450 VA. A UPQC-Q designed for a 20 % sag therefore needs about 3450 VA in each converter, 6900 VA in total, although the operating total during the sag is only 3450 VA (Example EX{ex_upqcq}). FIG{upqc_norm} gives the series-converter VA of both strategies over a wider range of sags. Harmonic and unbalance compensation add RMS current and voltage that must also enter the thermal ratings, so fundamental kVA alone understates the installed requirement.

### 9.5.4 Left-shunt UPQC analysis

In a left-shunt UPQC the series converter carries the load current, so zero-real-power series operation needs injection perpendicular to $I_L$, exactly as for the standalone DVR, and is governed by EQ{zpcond} and EQ{roots}. The shunt converter works at the disturbed PCC voltage $V_s'$ and supplies the load's reactive current referred to the supply: if $\beta=\phi-\theta_L$ is the angle between $V_s'$ and $I_L$, then $I_{sh}=I_L\sin\beta$ and $S_{sh}=V_s'I_L\sin\beta$. Unlike the right-shunt UPQC-Q, this arrangement can correct a swell with zero series real power, because the root of EQ{zpcond} with $\theta_L<0$ rotates the load voltage behind the supply and the injection then has a component opposing $V_s'$ (Example EX{ex_ls_swell}).

### 9.5.5 Control of the UPQC

FIG{upqc_ctrl1} shows the usual coordinated structure. The series converter uses the DVR controller of Section 9.4.4: an SRF voltage reference built from the PLL angle, $v_{inj}^{*}=v_L^{*}-v_s$, with load-voltage feedback and PWM voltage control. The target may preserve the pre-sag phase or deliberately rotate the load voltage (UPQC-Q, UPQC-S), and the synchronization and reference extraction must reflect that choice. Unbalanced regulation needs phase or sequence controllers rather than one positive-sequence dq magnitude.

The shunt converter uses the DSTATCOM controller of Section 9.3.3: $i_d^{*}=\bar i_{Ld}+i_{loss}$, $i_q^{*}=0$ and $i_{sh}^{*}=i_L-i_s^{*}$, with hysteresis or PWM current control. The DC-voltage PI output $i_{loss}$ now absorbs not only losses but also the series converter's real-power exchange. A feedforward term, scaling $\bar i_{Ld}$ by the ratio of rated to measured supply voltage, makes the source current rise immediately when a sag starts instead of waiting for the DC voltage to fall, and greatly reduces the DC-link dip. Both converters share the PLL angle.

![FIGDEF{upqc_ctrl1} Coordinated UPQC control: series voltage loop, shunt current loop and common DC-link regulation.](fig/image39.png){width=88%}

The two controllers act on different variables but share the DC link, and poorly separated bandwidths make them fight: a fast shunt DC-voltage loop may react to the very transient power that the series loop is deliberately producing. A robust hierarchy (FIG{upqc_ctrl2}) uses fast series-voltage and shunt-current loops, slower DC-link energy control, and a supervisor that selects UPQC-P, UPQC-Q or an optimized operating point. Current and voltage saturation must be coordinated as well. When the source voltage becomes very low, holding rated load voltage can demand both excessive series voltage and excessive source current, so the supervisor should reduce the restoration target or shed noncritical load before semiconductor limits are reached. This matters most in microgrids, where source strength can be low and PLL dynamics significant.

![FIGDEF{upqc_ctrl2} Layered control structure of a practical UPQC, from switching-level limits to the supervisory mode selection.](fig/f9_upqc_ctrl.png){width=88%}

### 9.5.6 Design of a three-phase four-wire UPQC

The design combines the DSTATCOM design of Section 9.3.4 with the DVR design of Section 9.4.6, using one DC bus for both [2].

*Shunt converter:* $V_{dc}$ from EQ{vdc}; $C_{dc}$ from EQ{cdc} (and EQ{csplit} if a split DC link carries the neutral current); $L_r$ from EQ{lr}; switch ratings from EQ{isw}. *Series converter:* the injection voltage covers the specified range, for example $\pm30$ % in-phase, $V_{DVR}=0.3\times239.6=71.9$ V per phase. Because both converters share the DC bus, the series converter's switches have the same voltage rating as the shunt converter's, and a step-up injection transformer is used, $K_{DVR}=V_{VSC}/V_{DVR}$. The series current is largest during the deepest sag, $I_s=P_L/[3(1-X)V_{ph}]$, and smallest during the largest swell; the inductor is sized at the smallest current because ripple is specified as a percentage of it:

$$L_{DVR}=\frac{(\sqrt3/2)\,mV_{dc}K_{DVR}}{6\,a\,f_s\,\Delta I_{DVR}}$$ EQDEF{ldvr}

### 9.5.7 Simulated performance

FIG{sim_upqc} shows an average-value simulation of a 415 V right-shunt UPQC-P supplying a six-pulse rectifier (30 A DC) in parallel with a 0.8 pf linear load, about 27.5 kW in total. The supply sags to 0.75 pu from 0.10 to 0.20 s and swells to 1.2 pu from 0.28 to 0.36 s. The load voltage stays at rated value. The source current is sinusoidal throughout and grows by a factor $1/(1-X)=1.33$ during the sag, as EQ{is} predicts, because the supply must deliver the same power at lower voltage. The shunt current carries the load harmonics and reactive current plus the real power that feeds the series converter. The DC-link voltage dips by about 15 V at the start of the sag and swells similarly at its end before the PI loop restores it.

### 9.5.8 Worked examples: UPQC

#### EXDEF{ex_upqcq}. Right-shunt UPQC-Q: ratings during sag and at nominal voltage

A single-phase right-shunt UPQC-Q supplies a 230 V, 50 Hz, 25 A, 0.8 lagging pf load and keeps the source at unity power factor. The supply sags by 20 %. Find the voltage, current and VA of each converter during the sag, and the installed ratings when nominal operation is included.

**Solution.** $P_L=230\times25\times0.8=4600$ W. Nominal: $V_{inj}=0$; the shunt converter supplies $I_L\sin\phi=15$ A at 230 V, 3450 VA. Sag: $V_s'=184$ V and $I_s=4600/184=25$ A. From EQ{upqcq}, $U=\sqrt{230^2-184^2}=138$ V and $\delta=36.87^\circ$; $S_{se}=138\times25=3450$ VA, all reactive. Since $\delta=\phi$, EQ{ishq} gives $I_{sh}=0$. Installed: series 138 V, 25 A, 3450 VA; shunt 230 V, 15 A, 3450 VA; total 6900 VA. The operating total during the sag is only 3450 VA, but each converter's rating is set by a different condition.

#### EXDEF{ex_upqcq10}. UPQC-Q at a shallower sag

A lossless right-shunt UPQC-Q protects a 230 V, 20 A, 0.8 pf load during a 10 % sag. Find the series and shunt duties.

**Solution.** $V_s'=207$ V, $P_L=3680$ W and $I_s=17.78$ A. $U=\sqrt{230^2-207^2}=100.25$ V, so $S_{se}=1782.3$ VA. $\delta=25.84^\circ$, $I_L=20\angle-11.03^\circ$ A relative to $V_s'$ and $|I_{sh}|=|I_L-I_s|=4.251$ A, so $S_{sh}=977.7$ VA. Total 2760.0 VA. For comparison, a standalone DVR protecting the same load at the same sag (FIG{roots}) needs only 863 VA at zero real power, because it carries a different current with a different geometry. Neither result supports a general claim that one arrangement always needs the larger rating.

#### EXDEF{ex_upqcp}. Right-shunt UPQC-P

For the load and sag of Example EX{ex_upqcq}, find the converter duties in UPQC-P mode.

**Solution.** $V_{inj}=230\times0.2=46$ V and $I_s=25$ A, so $S_{se}=P_{se}=1150$ VA. With $I_L=20-j15$ A, $I_{sh}=I_L-I_s=-5-j15$ A and $|I_{sh}|=15.81$ A, matching EQ{ishp}: $25\sqrt{0.36+0.64\times0.0625}$. $S_{sh}=230\times15.81=3636.6$ VA and $P_{sh}=-1150$ W, returning the series converter's export. Total 4786.6 VA. The series duty is a third of the UPQC-Q value, but the total is larger, because the shunt converter carries both the load's reactive current and the circulated real power.

#### EXDEF{ex_upqcq25}. UPQC-Q at a 25 % sag

A right-shunt UPQC-Q supplies 230 V, 20 A, 0.8 pf. The supply sags by 25 %. Find all ratings.

**Solution.** $P_L=3680$ W, $V_s'=172.5$ V and $I_s=21.33$ A. $U=152.13$ V and $S_{se}=3245.5$ VA. $\delta=41.41^\circ$, $\cos(\phi-\delta)=0.99686$, and EQ{ishq} gives $I_{sh}=2.111$ A and $S_{sh}=485.5$ VA during the sag. At nominal voltage the shunt converter needs $20\times0.6=12$ A, 2760 VA, which governs. Installed total: $3245.5+2760=6005.5$ VA.

#### EXDEF{ex_upqcs}. UPQC-S with a limited series voltage

A single-phase right-shunt UPQC protects a 230 V, 25 A, 0.8 pf load against a 30 % sag. The series converter is rated for 130 V. Find the injection angle, its real and reactive powers, the load-voltage rotation and the shunt duty, and compare with UPQC-Q and UPQC-P.

**Solution.** $V_s'=161$ V and $I_s=4600/161=28.57$ A. From EQ{psi}, $\cos\psi=(230^2-161^2-130^2)/(2\times161\times130)=0.2408$ and $\psi=76.07^\circ$. $P_{se}=130\times28.57\times0.2408=894.3$ W, $Q_{se}=3605.0$ var and $S_{se}=3714.3$ VA. $\delta=\sin^{-1}(130\sin76.07^\circ/230)=33.27^\circ$. $I_{sh}=|25\angle-3.60^\circ-28.57|=3.95$ A, so $S_{sh}=907.7$ VA and the total is 4621.9 VA. UPQC-Q at the same sag: $U=164.25$ V, $S_{se}=4692.9$ VA, $S_{sh}=1242.9$ VA, total 5935.9 VA. UPQC-P: $U=69$ V, $S_{se}=1971.4$ VA, $S_{sh}=3973.5$ VA, total 5945.0 VA. The UPQC-S angle reduces both the series voltage (130 V instead of 164 V) and the total operating VA, by about 22 %.

#### EXDEF{ex_ls_sag}. Left-shunt UPQC-Q during a sag

A left-shunt UPQC-Q supplies 230 V, 25 A, 0.8 pf with a 10 % sag. The series converter injects perpendicular to the load current, and the shunt converter keeps unity pf at the source. Find both duties during the sag and the installed ratings.

**Solution.** $V_s'=207$ V. From EQ{roots}, $\cos(\theta_L-\phi)=184/207=0.8889$, so $\theta_L=9.60^\circ$ (smaller root) and $V_{inj}=43.17\angle62.73^\circ$ V. The series converter carries $I_L=25$ A: $S_{se}=1079.2$ VA, purely reactive. $\beta=\phi-\theta_L=27.27^\circ$, $I_s=4600/207=22.22$ A and $I_{sh}=25\sin27.27^\circ=11.45$ A at 207 V, $S_{sh}=2370.8$ VA. At nominal voltage the shunt converter needs 15 A at 230 V, 3450 VA. Installed: $1079.2+3450=4529.2$ VA.

#### EXDEF{ex_ls_swell}. Left-shunt UPQC-Q during a swell

For the left-shunt UPQC of Example EX{ex_ls_sag}, the supply swells by 10 %. Find the duties.

**Solution.** $V_s'=253$ V and $\cos(\theta_L-\phi)=184/253=0.7273$, so $\theta_L-\phi=\pm43.34^\circ$. The root $\theta_L=-6.47^\circ$ (load voltage lagging the supply) gives $V_{inj}=35.65\angle-133.34^\circ$ V; the other root needs 311.6 V. $S_{se}=35.65\times25=891.2$ VA. $\beta=43.34^\circ$, so $I_{sh}=25\sin43.34^\circ=17.16$ A at 253 V and $S_{sh}=4341.2$ VA. Installed: shunt 253 V and 17.16 A (4341 VA), series 891 VA, total 5232 VA.

### 9.5.9 Advanced and multi-converter UPQC structures

The two-converter UPQC is the reference structure, but modern distribution systems motivate extensions (TAB{upqc_ext}). UPQC-DG connects distributed generation or storage to the common DC link so that the converters perform power-quality compensation and controlled active-power exchange together. An interline or multi-converter UPQC coordinates more than one feeder through a common DC link. Open and distributed UPQCs separate the converter locations while keeping coordinated objectives. Modular multilevel and cascaded converters extend the voltage rating and improve waveform quality at medium voltage. None of these is automatically superior: adding a PV array, battery or extra feeder changes the DC-link power balance, protection, islanding behaviour and converter rating, so a design must state which services are simultaneous, which are mutually exclusive under current limit, and how energy management interacts with fast power-quality control [24].

Table: TABDEF{upqc_ext} Extensions of the UPQC.

| Extension | Added capability | New design issue |
|:--|:--|:--|
| UPQC-DG / PV-UPQC | PQ compensation plus generation interface | DC-link energy management and curtailment |
| UPQC-BESS | Sag support, peak shaving, ride-through | State of charge, battery power limit and protection |
| Interline / MC-UPQC | Coordination of several feeders | Cross-feeder real-power balance and fault isolation |
| Open / distributed UPQC | Flexible placement, less common hardware | Communication, synchronization and protection |
| MMC / cascaded UPQC | Medium-voltage scalability and lower $dv/dt$ | Cell balancing, stored energy and fault handling |
