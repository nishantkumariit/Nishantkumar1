## 9.1 From Transmission FACTS to Distribution Custom Power

Chapters 6 to 8 used voltage-source converters to control power flow, voltage and stability on transmission networks. When the same converter technology is applied inside a distribution system, at a customer bus or a feeder, the goal changes. The converter now protects a load from a bad supply, or protects the supply from a bad load. Hingorani called this family custom power [1]. Three members dominate practice and give this chapter its structure: the distribution static compensator (DSTATCOM), the dynamic voltage restorer (DVR) and the unified power quality conditioner (UPQC). Active power filters (APFs), solid-state transfer switches and fault-current limiters belong to the same family.

The three main devices differ by how they connect (FIG{families}). A DSTATCOM is connected in shunt and injects current, so it naturally corrects problems that originate in the load current: poor power factor, unbalance, neutral current and harmonics. A DVR is connected in series through an injection transformer and inserts voltage, so it corrects problems in the supply voltage: sags, swells, unbalance, notches and voltage harmonics. A UPQC places a series converter and a shunt converter on a common DC link and can do both jobs, subject to the ratings of each converter and the energy available on the shared link.

![FIGDEF{families} Connection families of custom power devices: (a) shunt, (b) series and (c) series-shunt.](fig/image1.png){width=92%}

Typical installations range from a few kVA at 230 V to several MVA at medium voltage. Neither voltage nor size defines the family; the purpose and control objective do. The series-voltage, shunt-current and DC-energy relationships of Chapters 6 to 8 remain valid, but distribution adds its own constraints: neutral conductors and zero-sequence current, single-phase and nonlinear loads, injection-transformer saturation, fault bypass, very short disturbance-detection times and the tolerance of the specific equipment being protected. A DVR controls protected-load voltage, while an SSSC mainly controls a line's effective series compensation; shared converter mathematics does not mean identical duties.

FIG{classif} summarizes how the three devices are classified in the literature [2], [3], [4]: by converter type (current-source or voltage-source), by supply system (single-phase two-wire, three-phase three-wire and three-phase four-wire), by topology, by the source of DC energy and, for the UPQC, by shunt location and by how the series converter shares real and reactive power.

![FIGDEF{classif} Classification of DSTATCOM, DVR and UPQC.](fig/image2.png){width=92%}

Voltage-source converters (VSCs) have largely displaced current-source converters (CSCs) for custom power. A CSC needs a large DC inductor and sizeable AC capacitors, has higher losses and is not easily extended to multilevel forms. A PWM VSC with a DC capacitor is lighter, cheaper, has lower losses and scales to higher ratings with multilevel structures. Unless stated otherwise, every device in this chapter uses a PWM VSC.

| Attribute | Transmission FACTS | Distribution custom power |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Purpose | Network power flow, voltage and stability | Voltage and current quality at a load, bus or feeder |

### 9.1.1 A physical view of custom power compensation

The natural entry point is to ask where the disturbance originates and which electrical quantity must be synthesized. If the load current is undesirable while the supply voltage is acceptable, the compensator should inject current in shunt. If the supply voltage is undesirable while the load current must continue, it should inject voltage in series. If both are unacceptable, the two actions can be coordinated through a common DC link. A shunt converter cannot directly create a missing series voltage, although its current can move the PCC voltage through the feeder impedance; a series converter cannot directly cancel a nonlinear load current at the source, although its voltage can shape that current indirectly. Network impedance, converter rating, available DC energy and control bandwidth therefore decide what can actually be corrected (TAB{location}).

Table: TABDEF{location} Compensation location and its principal physical limit.

| Location | Controlled quantity | Primary disturbance | Typical device | Principal physical limit |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Shunt | Injected current | Reactive, harmonic, unbalanced or neutral load current | DSTATCOM / shunt APF | Converter current and PCC voltage |
| Series | Injected voltage | Sag, swell, unbalance, voltage harmonics | DVR / series APF | Injection voltage, line current and stored energy |
| Series + shunt | Voltage and current together | Simultaneous supply and load problems | UPQC | Both converter ratings and common DC-link balance |
| Multi-feeder | Coordinated series voltages | Sag support across several feeders | IDVR / MC-UPQC | Cross-feeder real-power balance and converter ratings |

### 9.1.2 From ideal compensator to practical converter

Each device is developed in four layers. The first is the ideal compensation law obtained from Kirchhoff's laws and phasors. The second is the converter realization: VSC topology, transformer or coupling inductor, DC link and filters. The third is the control system that estimates the disturbance and forces the converter to reproduce the required voltage or current. The fourth is the practical envelope set by semiconductor current, modulation voltage, stored energy, thermal limits, protection and the upstream network. This layering guards against a common error in custom-power studies, in which a controller computes a mathematically perfect reference that the converter cannot produce. Every derivation in the chapter therefore ends with a feasibility check.

## 9.2 Power-Quality Problems in Distribution Systems

### 9.2.1 Voltage-quality and current-quality problems

It is useful to sort distribution power-quality problems by where they originate. A voltage-quality problem is already present in the supply voltage before the protected load sees it: a sag caused by a remote fault, a swell on the healthy phases during a single-line-to-ground fault on an ungrounded or impedance-grounded system, voltage unbalance, notching caused by a neighbour's converter, flicker or voltage harmonics. A current-quality problem is created by the load and pushed back into the network: reactive current, unbalanced phase currents, excessive neutral current and harmonic current. Current-quality problems become voltage-quality problems for other customers once the current flows through the network impedance (TAB{vq_cq}).

Table: TABDEF{vq_cq} Voltage-quality and current-quality problems and the natural compensator.

| Voltage-quality problems (supply side) | Current-quality problems (load side) |
|:--|:--|
| Sag, swell, interruption | Poor displacement power factor (reactive current) |
| Voltage unbalance | Unbalanced phase currents |
| Notching, flicker, fluctuation | Excessive neutral current (triplen harmonics, single-phase loads) |
| Voltage harmonics, transients | Harmonic currents, load fluctuation |
| Natural compensator: series (DVR) | Natural compensator: shunt (DSTATCOM, shunt APF) |

### 9.2.2 Disturbance categories

FIG{disturb} shows eight commonly encountered disturbances; it is not an exhaustive classification. Under IEEE Std 1159-2019 [5], a short-duration voltage sag has an RMS magnitude of 0.1 to 0.9 pu lasting from half a cycle to one minute. A swell starts at about 1.1 pu, and its typical upper magnitude depends on duration. An interruption has an RMS voltage below 0.1 pu; a sustained interruption lasts longer than one minute. Harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental, usually generated by nonlinear loads. Notches are short, periodic voltage dips produced when a line-commutated converter momentarily shorts two phases during commutation. Voltage fluctuations at a few hertz produce visible lamp flicker. Transients may be impulsive (lightning) or oscillatory (capacitor switching). Unbalance, interharmonics, DC offset, noise and frequency variation also matter.

### 9.2.3 Reading a voltage-tolerance curve

Equipment sensitivity to sags and swells is commonly summarized by a tolerance curve such as the one published by the Information Technology Industry Council (ITI), historically the CBEMA curve [6]. FIG{iti} reproduces only its general shape. The ITI curve is a specific reference for 120 V, 60 Hz information-technology equipment, and its application note should be consulted for exact coordinates. For a real installation, use the protected equipment's own ride-through specification at its actual supply voltage and frequency.

The curve plots acceptable voltage against disturbance duration. A brief, deep sag may be tolerated, while the same depth held longer causes a trip. Duration, and not only depth, is therefore a first-class design parameter throughout the chapter: it sets the DVR's stored energy and the response time its controller must achieve.

### 9.2.4 Power-quality indices

Several indices recur in this chapter. For a periodic quantity with RMS harmonic components $X_h$, total harmonic distortion is

$$\mathrm{THD}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{h\ge2}X_h^{2}}}{X_1}\times100\ \%$$ EQDEF{thd}

where $X_1$ is the RMS fundamental. Total demand distortion (TDD) has the same numerator but uses the maximum demand load current as denominator (Section 9.7). For a load with RMS fundamental current $I_1$, displacement angle $\phi_1$ and total RMS current $I$, the true power factor separates into a displacement factor and a distortion factor:

$$\mathrm{PF}=\frac{P}{VI}=\frac{I_1}{I}\cos\phi_1=\frac{\cos\phi_1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{THD}_I^{2}}}$$ EQDEF{pf}

with $\mathrm{THD}_I$ as a fraction, assuming a sinusoidal voltage. A capacitor bank can only correct $\cos\phi_1$; a DSTATCOM can correct both factors. Voltage unbalance is most rigorously expressed by the voltage unbalance factor,

$$\mathrm{VUF}=\frac{|V_n|}{|V_p|}\times100\ \%$$ EQDEF{vuf}

where $V_p$ and $V_n$ are the positive- and negative-sequence components of Chapter 2.

### 9.2.5 Matching problems to devices

A DVR corrects load-voltage sags and swells within its voltage, current, energy and control limits. A DSTATCOM or shunt APF compensates load-generated reactive, unbalanced, neutral and harmonic currents, and through the feeder impedance can also regulate the bus voltage. A UPQC combines these services. A UPQC can replenish the series converter's real power from the grid during a sag only while adequate source voltage remains; with the source absent, it needs usable stored energy and a topology that can safely supply an isolated load (Section 9.8.2). Flicker mitigation needs enough bandwidth and current or voltage margin. Very fast impulsive transients usually need surge protection, not a converter (TAB{candidates}).

Table: TABDEF{candidates} Power-quality problems and candidate devices.

| Dominant problem | Candidate device and required checks |
|:--|:--|
| Low power factor, reactive demand | DSTATCOM in UPF mode; capacitor bank if the load is steady and linear |
| Feeder voltage regulation | DSTATCOM in ZVR mode; voltage effect depends on feeder impedance |
| Unbalanced load, neutral current | Three-phase four-wire DSTATCOM (four-leg, split-capacitor or transformer-assisted) |
| Load-current harmonics | DSTATCOM / shunt APF; hybrid filter after a resonance study |
| Voltage sag or swell | DVR with enough injection voltage, current, energy and phase-transition capability |
| Sags on several feeders | Interline DVR, if joint operating points and shared-link balance are feasible |
| Supply-voltage harmonics, notches | DVR or series APF designed for harmonic voltage injection |
| Both voltage and current problems | UPQC, subject to both converter duties, source capacity and DC balance |
| Sustained interruption | UPS, standby source or island-capable storage scheme with isolation |

## 9.3 Distribution Static Compensator (DSTATCOM)

The DSTATCOM is a shunt-connected VSC whose job is to make the current drawn from the supply look like the current of an ideal load: sinusoidal, balanced and, if required, in phase with the voltage. It is the distribution counterpart of the STATCOM of Chapter 6 [19], but its duty is wider. A transmission STATCOM mainly exchanges positive-sequence fundamental reactive power; a DSTATCOM is commonly asked to cancel harmonics, balance single-phase loads and remove neutral current as well. Commercial units range from a few hundred kVA to several MVA at medium voltage, often coordinating switched capacitor or reactor banks.

### 9.3.1 Operating principle

FIG{dst_sld} shows the single-line arrangement. The VSC is connected at the point of common coupling (PCC) through an interfacing inductor $L_r$, and a small $R_f$-$C_f$ ripple filter at the PCC absorbs switching-frequency current. The DC side has only a capacitor; no external energy source is needed because, ideally, the DSTATCOM exchanges no average real power. Voltage and current sensors feed a DSP or rapid-prototyping controller that generates the gate pulses.

![FIGDEF{dst_sld} DSTATCOM single-line diagram with interfacing inductor, ripple filter and DC-link capacitor.](fig/image5.png){width=80%}

With the compensator current $i_c$ defined positive from the VSC into the PCC, Kirchhoff's current law gives

$$i_s=i_L-i_c$$ EQDEF{kcl}

so whatever part of the load current the DSTATCOM supplies locally no longer flows from the source. Decompose the load current of phase $k$ into four parts:

$$i_{Lk}=i_{pk}+i_{qk}+i_{uk}+i_{hk}$$ EQDEF{decomp}

where $i_{pk}$ is the fundamental positive-sequence current in phase with the voltage (it carries the load's average real power), $i_{qk}$ the fundamental positive-sequence current in quadrature, $i_{uk}$ the negative- and zero-sequence fundamental components and $i_{hk}$ the harmonics. In full compensation the source reference is $i_{sk}^{*}=i_{pk}+i_{loss,k}$, where $i_{loss}$ is a small in-phase current that supplies converter losses and holds the DC-link voltage, and the DSTATCOM must inject $i_{ck}^{*}=i_{qk}+i_{uk}+i_{hk}-i_{loss,k}$. Which components remain in the source current is a design choice: in unity-power-factor operation only $i_p$ remains, while in zero-voltage-regulation operation a deliberate fundamental reactive component is retained so that the feeder drop yields the required PCC magnitude.

Treating the VSC fundamental output as a voltage $V_c\angle\delta$ behind a reactance $X_r$, with PCC voltage $V\angle0$, the power delivered to the PCC per phase is

$$P_c=\frac{VV_c\sin\delta}{X_r},\qquad Q_c=\frac{V\left(V_c\cos\delta-V\right)}{X_r}$$ EQDEF{pcqc}

When $V_c\cos\delta>V$ the converter delivers reactive power (capacitive operation); when $V_c\cos\delta<V$ it absorbs it (inductive operation). A small angle $\delta$ draws real power to charge the DC capacitor and cover losses. In practice the converter runs in current-controlled PWM, so these relations describe what the current controller achieves rather than how it is commanded.

The same hardware supports several objectives, often simultaneously:

- *Power-factor correction or unity power factor (UPF) mode.* Source current is forced into phase with the PCC voltage, so the source supplies only real power. The PCC voltage settles wherever the feeder impedance puts it.
- *Zero voltage regulation (ZVR) mode.* The DSTATCOM injects enough reactive current to hold the PCC voltage magnitude at its reference. The source current then contains a reactive component, so the source power factor is no longer unity.
- *Load balancing.* Negative-sequence and, in four-wire systems, zero-sequence load currents are supplied locally, so the source sees a balanced set.
- *Harmonic elimination.* Load harmonic currents are supplied locally; this is shunt active filtering.
- *Neutral-current compensation.* In a four-wire system the zero-sequence and triplen currents return through the compensator's own neutral path, so the source neutral carries almost nothing.

FIG{dst_phasor} compares the three cases that matter most for fundamental quantities. Without compensation, the lagging load current produces a drop across the feeder and the load voltage falls. In UPF mode the source current is aligned with the PCC voltage; the drop $jX_sI_s$ is now in quadrature with $V_L$, so the magnitude falls less but still falls. In ZVR mode the source current is advanced until $|V_L|=|V_s|$.

![FIGDEF{dst_phasor} Phasor diagrams: (a) uncompensated lagging load, (b) DSTATCOM in UPF mode, (c) DSTATCOM in ZVR mode.](fig/image6.png){width=90%}

*Reactive current needed for ZVR.* Take the PCC voltage $V_L$ as reference and the source EMF magnitude $|V_s|$ as given. With per-phase load real power $P$ and a lossless compensator, the source current is $I_s=I_p+jI_q$ with $I_p=P/V_L$. KVL across the feeder impedance $Z_s=R_s+jX_s$ gives $V_s=V_L+Z_sI_s$, and requiring $|V_s|$ to equal the specified source magnitude leads to a quadratic in $I_q$:

$$\left(R_s^{2}+X_s^{2}\right)I_q^{2}+2\left(BR_s-AX_s\right)I_q+A^{2}+B^{2}-|V_s|^{2}=0,\qquad A=V_L+R_sI_p,\ B=X_sI_p$$ EQDEF{zvr}

The smaller root, normally positive (leading source current), is used. The compensator current then follows from $I_c=I_L-I_s$ in each phase. ZVR is only as good as the feeder impedance allows: on a stiff bus the reactive current needed to move the voltage becomes large, while on a weak bus a small current has a large effect. A controller that ignores $R_s$ may work on an inductive medium-voltage feeder but decouples poorly on low-voltage feeders where $R/X$ is appreciable. Example EX{ex_zvr} works through a complete case.

*DSTATCOM compared with an SVC.* Chapter 6 showed that an SVC behaves as a controlled susceptance, so its maximum capacitive current falls in proportion to voltage. A DSTATCOM is current-limited: it can hold rated capacitive current down to very low PCC voltage (FIG{vi}). During the voltage dips that distribution customers care most about, this matters. The DSTATCOM also responds within a fraction of a cycle, injects harmonics and negative sequence on command, and does not create the low-order resonances that capacitor banks can.

![FIGDEF{vi} V-I characteristic of a DSTATCOM compared with an SVC (schematic). Along the droop line the bus voltage rises as the compensator moves from capacitive to inductive current.](fig/f9_vi.png){width=66%}

### 9.3.2 Classification and topologies

*Converter type.* A current-fed DSTATCOM uses a CSC with a DC inductor; a voltage-fed DSTATCOM uses a VSC with a DC capacitor. For the reasons given in Section 9.1, VSCs dominate.

*Three-phase three-wire topologies.* FIG{topo3} shows the four common arrangements. The three-leg VSC (a) uses six switches and one DC capacitor and is the standard choice. The two-leg VSC with split capacitors (b) saves two switches by tying the third phase to the capacitor midpoint, at the cost of a higher DC voltage and capacitor-voltage balancing. Three single-phase H-bridges (c) each need an isolation transformer but give independent phase control and redundancy. An isolating star/delta transformer (d) matches a low-voltage converter to a medium-voltage bus and provides galvanic isolation.

![FIGDEF{topo3} Three-phase three-wire DSTATCOM topologies.](fig/image8.png){width=92%}

*Three-phase four-wire topologies.* Four-wire distribution feeds single-phase loads between line and neutral, so zero-sequence current flows and triplen harmonics add in the neutral. A six-switch three-leg bridge cannot synthesize zero-sequence current, so a neutral path must be added (FIG{topo4}). The four-leg VSC (a) adds a fourth leg with its own inductor $L_{rn}$ and controls the neutral current directly. The split-capacitor three-leg VSC (b) returns the neutral current through the capacitor midpoint; it needs no extra switches but large, balanced capacitors because the neutral current flows through them. Transformer-assisted schemes (c) and (d) let a three-leg VSC handle positive and negative sequence while a passive transformer provides a low-impedance zero-sequence path. Zig-zag, star/delta, T-connected, star/hexagon, star/polygon and Scott-connected transformers have all been used [14]. Three single-phase H-bridges with isolation transformers are also used. TAB{topo_cmp} summarizes the trade-offs.

![FIGDEF{topo4} Three-phase four-wire DSTATCOM topologies.](fig/image9.png){width=92%}

Table: TABDEF{topo_cmp} Four-wire DSTATCOM topologies compared.

| Topology | Zero-sequence capability | Main advantage | Main design concern |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Three-leg VSC | No direct neutral-current path | Lowest switch count for three-wire systems | Cannot compensate feeder neutral current |
| Four-leg VSC | Full controlled path | Independent neutral-current control | Extra leg, three-dimensional modulation and current limit |
| Split DC link | Neutral tied to midpoint | No fourth leg | Capacitor-voltage imbalance and ripple |
| Zig-zag assisted | Passive zero-sequence path | Reduces converter neutral duty | Transformer size, losses and harmonic heating |

The zig-zag transformer works because each limb carries two windings belonging to different phases, connected in opposition (FIG{zigzag}). Zero-sequence currents are equal in all three phases, so the two mmfs on each limb cancel and the zero-sequence current sees only leakage impedance. Positive- and negative-sequence currents do not cancel and see the full magnetizing impedance, so the transformer draws almost nothing from the balanced system. Connected at the load, it diverts most of the neutral current away from the source. Its effectiveness depends on its zero-sequence impedance relative to the source neutral path, so it is usually combined with active control rather than relied on alone.

### 9.3.3 Control algorithms

A DSTATCOM controller has three layers. The reference generator extracts from measured voltages and currents the source-current reference $i_s^{*}$ (indirect current control) or the compensator-current reference $i_c^{*}=i_L-i_s^{*}$ (direct current control). A DC-link voltage regulator, usually a PI controller, adds the small active current $i_{loss}$ that keeps $v_{dc}$ at its reference. A current controller, hysteresis or PWM, makes the VSC track the reference. The current loop must be substantially faster than the outer voltage loops: an outer DC-voltage loop that is too fast tries to correct switching-scale and twice-frequency energy pulsations and injects distortion into the reference, while a slow current loop cannot reproduce harmonic commands. Bandwidth separation is therefore a physical requirement, not a tuning preference.

Many reference-generation algorithms have been published [2], [13]. In the time domain they include unit-template (PI-based) control, the power balance theory, the $I\cos\phi$ algorithm, current synchronous detection, instantaneous reactive power (pq) theory, synchronous reference frame (SRF or dq) theory, instantaneous symmetrical component theory, single-phase pq and dq theories, Adaline (LMS) estimators and enhanced-PLL methods. In the frequency domain they include Fourier, DFT and recursive-DFT extraction, Kalman filtering and wavelet methods. The four most widely taught are developed below; the others are summarized in TAB{alg_cmp}.

#### Unit-template (PI-controller-based) control

This is the simplest scheme (FIG{unit_tpl}). The amplitude of the PCC voltage is estimated from the three sampled phase voltages,

$$V_t=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}\left(v_{sa}^{2}+v_{sb}^{2}+v_{sc}^{2}\right)}$$ EQDEF{vt}

and in-phase unit templates are formed as $u_a=v_{sa}/V_t$, $u_b=v_{sb}/V_t$, $u_c=v_{sc}/V_t$. For balanced sinusoidal voltages these are unit-amplitude sinusoids locked to the supply. Quadrature unit templates, each leading its in-phase template by $90^\circ$, follow algebraically:

$$w_a=\frac{-u_b+u_c}{\sqrt3},\quad w_b=\frac{\sqrt3u_a}{2}+\frac{u_b-u_c}{2\sqrt3},\quad w_c=-\frac{\sqrt3u_a}{2}+\frac{u_b-u_c}{2\sqrt3}$$ EQDEF{wtpl}

A PI controller acting on the DC-voltage error gives the amplitude $I_{sp}^{*}$ of the in-phase source current. It settles automatically at the load's real-power current plus losses: if the source supplies too little, the capacitor makes up the difference, $v_{dc}$ falls and the PI output rises. In ZVR mode a second PI acting on $V_t^{*}-V_t$ gives the quadrature amplitude $I_{sq}^{*}$. The source-current references are

$$i_{sk}^{*}=I_{sp}^{*}u_k+I_{sq}^{*}w_k,\qquad k=a,b,c$$ EQDEF{utref}

with $I_{sq}^{*}=0$ in UPF mode. The method is simple and cheap, but the DC-voltage loop must be slow to reject its own ripple, so the source current settles over a few cycles after a load step, and distorted or unbalanced PCC voltage contaminates the templates. Filtering the templates or using a PLL removes the second problem.

![FIGDEF{unit_tpl} Unit-template (PI-controller-based) reference generation for UPF and ZVR modes.](fig/image11.png){width=86%}

#### Synchronous reference frame (SRF, dq) theory

The SRF method transforms the load currents into a frame rotating with the positive-sequence fundamental voltage, where that component appears as DC [22]. With $\theta$ from a PLL locked to the PCC voltage, the amplitude-invariant Park transformation is

$$\begin{bmatrix}i_{Ld}\\ i_{Lq}\\ i_{L0}\end{bmatrix}=\frac23\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\cos\left(\theta-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)&\cos\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)\\ -\sin\theta&-\sin\left(\theta-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)&-\sin\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)\\ \frac12&\frac12&\frac12\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}i_{La}\\ i_{Lb}\\ i_{Lc}\end{bmatrix}$$ EQDEF{park}

With voltage $v_a=V_m\cos\theta$, a fundamental positive-sequence current $I_m\cos(\theta-\phi)$ maps to $i_{Ld}=I_m\cos\phi$ and $i_{Lq}=-I_m\sin\phi$, both constant: a lagging load has negative $i_{Lq}$ in this convention. Harmonics and negative sequence appear as ripple; for a six-pulse rectifier on a 50 Hz supply the 5th and 7th harmonics both map to 300 Hz in the dq frame. A low-pass filter, or a moving average over one sixth of a cycle for balanced six-pulse loads, extracts the DC part $\bar i_{Ld}$. The references are then (FIG{srf})

$$i_d^{*}=\bar i_{Ld}+i_{loss},\qquad i_q^{*}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{UPF mode}\\ i_{qr},&\text{ZVR mode}\end{cases}$$ EQDEF{srfref}

where $i_{loss}$ is the output of the DC-voltage PI and $i_{qr}$ that of an AC-voltage PI. The inverse transformation $i_{sa}^{*}=i_d^{*}\cos\theta-i_q^{*}\sin\theta$ (and likewise for $b$ and $c$ with $\theta\mp2\pi/3$) returns balanced, sinusoidal source-current references. A positive $i_q^{*}$ is a leading source current, which raises the PCC voltage through the feeder inductance. Because the reference is built from the PLL angle rather than from the measured voltage waveform, SRF control gives clean sinusoidal source currents even when the PCC voltage is distorted. Its accuracy depends on the PLL and on the low-pass filter, which trades dynamic response against ripple rejection.

![FIGDEF{srf} SRF (dq) control of a three-leg DSTATCOM.](fig/image12.png){width=92%}

#### Instantaneous reactive power (pq) theory

Akagi, Kanazawa and Nabae's instantaneous power theory [15], [7] works in the stationary $\alpha\beta$ frame. The first step applies the Clarke transformation to voltages and currents (FIG{clarke}):

$$\begin{bmatrix}i_\alpha\\ i_\beta\end{bmatrix}=\frac23\begin{bmatrix}1&-\frac12&-\frac12\\ 0&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}i_a\\ i_b\\ i_c\end{bmatrix}$$ EQDEF{clarke}

The $2/3$ factor is amplitude-invariant: balanced currents of peak $I_m$ trace an $\alpha\beta$ circle of radius $I_m$. With zero sequence absent, true instantaneous three-phase power is $P_{3\phi}=\frac{3}{2}p$; a power-invariant transform uses a different scaling.

![FIGDEF{clarke} Amplitude-invariant Clarke axes.](fig/image13.png){width=46%}

The instantaneous real and imaginary powers are

$$p=v_\alpha i_\alpha+v_\beta i_\beta,\qquad q=v_\alpha i_\beta-v_\beta i_\alpha$$ EQDEF{pq}

With the positive-$\beta$ axis and cosine phase sequence used here, a lagging balanced load has $q<0$ and its conventional reactive power is $Q=-\frac{3}{2}\bar q$; this sign must be carried into the inverse and into the controller. For balanced sinusoidal voltages, a linear balanced load gives constant $p$ and $q$; harmonics and unbalance in the current produce oscillating parts. Distorted or unbalanced voltage also produces oscillating power, so practical controllers often build the current reference from the extracted positive-sequence fundamental voltage.

Split each power into an average and an oscillating part, $p=\bar p+\tilde p$ and $q=\bar q+\tilde q$. To cancel harmonics only, the compensator powers are $p_C=\tilde p$ and $q_C=\tilde q$. To cancel harmonics and fundamental reactive current, $p_C=\tilde p$ and $q_C=q$. A DC-voltage regulator adds a small term to $p_C$ to cover losses. Inverting EQ{pq} gives

$$i_{\alpha,C}=\frac{v_\alpha p_C-v_\beta q_C}{v_\alpha^{2}+v_\beta^{2}},\qquad i_{\beta,C}=\frac{v_\beta p_C+v_\alpha q_C}{v_\alpha^{2}+v_\beta^{2}}$$ EQDEF{pqinv}

and the phase references follow from the inverse amplitude-invariant transformation,

$$\begin{bmatrix}i_{C,a}\\ i_{C,b}\\ i_{C,c}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\ -\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&1\\ -\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}i_{\alpha,C}\\ i_{\beta,C}\\ i_{0,C}\end{bmatrix}$$ EQDEF{iclarke}

with $i_{0,C}=0$ in a three-wire installation. The denominator in EQ{pqinv} must be kept away from zero during deep sags; a practical controller saturates the current, gives priority to the most important duty and has a declared fallback. FIG{pqchain} shows the complete signal chain.

![FIGDEF{pqchain} pq-based compensating-current reference.](fig/image14.png){width=92%}

*Four-wire extension.* A four-wire load can have a nonzero phase-current sum. Extend the transformation with

$$i_0=\frac{1}{3}\left(i_a+i_b+i_c\right),\qquad v_0=\frac{1}{3}\left(v_a+v_b+v_c\right)$$ EQDEF{zeroseq}

For neutral current defined as returning from the load, $i_n=i_a+i_b+i_c=3i_0$, and the complete real-power identity is

$$P_{3\phi}=\frac{3}{2}\left(v_\alpha i_\alpha+v_\beta i_\beta\right)+3v_0i_0$$ EQDEF{p3ph}

A four-wire DSTATCOM commands $i_{0,C}=i_{0,L}$ so that the source carries no zero sequence. When $v_0=0$ this term must be controlled directly as a current; it cannot be reconstructed by dividing a zero-sequence power by $v_0$, because nonzero $i_0$ can exist with zero $p_0$. A three-wire bridge without a neutral path cannot deliver zero-sequence current at all.

#### Other algorithms in brief

Power balance theory computes the instantaneous load power $p_L=\sum v_{sk}i_{Lk}$, low-pass filters it and divides by the voltage amplitude to obtain the in-phase source-current amplitude, and does the same with an instantaneous reactive power for ZVR. $I\cos\phi$ control extracts, at the zero crossings of each phase voltage, the in-phase and quadrature magnitudes of that phase's load current, averages them over the three phases and multiplies by the unit templates. Current synchronous detection is closely related and works with filtered positive-sequence voltages. Instantaneous symmetrical component theory forces balanced source currents with a chosen power-factor angle $\phi_p$ directly:

$$i_{sa}^{*}=\frac{v_{sa}+\beta\left(v_{sb}-v_{sc}\right)}{\sum_kv_{sk}^{2}}\,\bar P_L,\qquad \beta=\frac{\tan\phi_p}{\sqrt3}$$ EQDEF{isc}

with $b$ and $c$ obtained cyclically and $\beta=0$ for unity power factor. Adaline (LMS) control treats the fundamental active component of each load current as a weight $W_p$ multiplying the unit template and updates it every sample:

$$W_{pa}(k+1)=W_{pa}(k)+\eta\left[i_{La}(k)-W_{pa}(k)u_a(k)\right]u_a(k)$$ EQDEF{lms}

where $\eta$ is a convergence factor, typically 0.001 to 0.1. Single-phase pq and dq theories construct the missing orthogonal signal by delaying each phase voltage and current by a quarter cycle, so each phase is compensated independently, which suits single-phase loads and three single-phase VSCs. Proportional-resonant controllers work in the stationary frame and track sinusoidal references without a dq transformation, while repetitive and selective-harmonic controllers provide high gain at periodic harmonic frequencies at the cost of one-cycle memory and robustness margin.

#### Current control, limiting and anti-windup

A hysteresis controller switches a phase leg whenever the actual current leaves a band around the reference. It is fast and independent of load parameters but has a variable switching frequency. A PWM current controller compares the current error, passed through a PI or resonant regulator, with a fixed-frequency carrier, which gives a known switching spectrum and easier filter design. Deadbeat, predictive and sliding-mode current controllers are also used. Whatever the method, the current-loop bandwidth must exceed the highest harmonic the DSTATCOM is expected to cancel, and the DC voltage must be high enough to drive $di_c/dt$ through $L_r$ at the steepest part of the reference.

During a severe load step or a depressed PCC voltage, the requested current can exceed the converter rating, and the reference must be limited before modulation. A useful priority is semiconductor safety first, DC-link stability second and the chosen power-quality objective third, although the exact order is application dependent. Anti-windup is needed in every PI loop because a saturated converter cannot realize the controller output. Harmonic cancellation, power-factor correction and voltage regulation compete for the same current, so a controller that claims all three must state how the current limit is shared when their references conflict.

### 9.3.4 Design of a DSTATCOM

The design of a three-leg VSC-based DSTATCOM covers the DC-bus voltage and capacitance, the interfacing inductor, the ripple filter and the switch ratings [2].

*DC-bus voltage.* With sinusoidal PWM, the peak phase voltage a three-leg VSC can produce is $mV_{dc}/2$. The DC voltage must therefore exceed twice the peak phase voltage of the PCC:

$$V_{dc}=\frac{2\sqrt2\,V_{LL}}{\sqrt3\,m}$$ EQDEF{vdc}

where $m$ is the modulation index, normally taken as 1. At 415 V this gives 677.7 V, and 700 V is a common choice. A slightly higher value gives headroom for ZVR operation and for the voltage across $L_r$.

*DC-bus capacitance.* The capacitor must supply the energy demanded during a load change until the DC-voltage loop responds. Equating the energy released by a permitted voltage drop from $V_{dc}$ to $V_{dc1}$ to the converter's energy over a recovery time $t$,

$$\frac{1}{2}C_{dc}\left(V_{dc}^{2}-V_{dc1}^{2}\right)=k\,3V_{ph}\left(aI\right)t$$ EQDEF{cdc}

where $V_{ph}$ is the phase voltage, $I$ the rated DSTATCOM phase current, $a$ an overload factor (about 1.2) and $k$ the fraction of rated power that must actually be buffered (1 for a conservative estimate). In a split-capacitor converter the neutral current flows into the capacitor midpoint and adds a fundamental-frequency ripple. With neutral-current amplitude $\hat I_n$ and two capacitors $C$ in series, the midpoint current divides between them, so the ripple on each capacitor is $\Delta v_{pp}=\hat I_n/(\omega C)$ and

$$C\ge\frac{\hat I_n}{\omega\,\Delta v_{pp}}$$ EQDEF{csplit}

A four-leg converter does not pass neutral current through its capacitor, but single-phase loading still produces a twice-frequency power pulsation that must be checked.

*Interfacing inductance.* The inductor limits the switching-frequency ripple in $i_c$. For a three-leg VSC with switching frequency $f_s$ and permitted peak-to-peak ripple $\Delta i_{cr}$,

$$L_r=\frac{\sqrt3\,mV_{dc}}{12\,a\,f_s\,\Delta i_{cr}}$$ EQDEF{lr}

Ripple is usually allowed to be 5 to 15 % of rated current. A larger $L_r$ reduces ripple but slows the current response and needs more DC voltage. For a single-phase H-bridge, or each bridge of a three-single-phase converter, the corresponding expression is $L_r=mV_{dc}/(4af_s\Delta i_{cr})$.

*Ripple filter.* A series $R_f$-$C_f$ branch at the PCC presents a low impedance at the switching frequency and a high impedance at 50 Hz. Its corner frequency is placed near half the switching frequency:

$$f_r=\frac{1}{2\pi R_fC_f}=\frac{f_s}{2}$$ EQDEF{rf}

This is the RC corner frequency, not a resonance; the branch is not tuned. With $R_f=5\ \Omega$ and $f_s=10$ kHz, $C_f=6.4\ \mu$F, and 10 $\mu$F is usually selected.

*Semiconductor ratings.* The device voltage rating covers the DC bus plus a dynamic overshoot of about 10 %, $V_{sw}=1.1V_{dc}$, and the next standard class above this (1200 V for a 700 V bus) is chosen. The current rating covers the peak of rated current plus the ripple, with a 25 % margin:

$$I_{sw}=1.25\left(\Delta i_{cr}+\sqrt2\,I_c\right)$$ EQDEF{isw}

Adding the full peak-to-peak ripple, as here, is slightly conservative; some texts add only half of it. A design should state its convention.

### 9.3.6 Worked examples: DSTATCOM

#### EXDEF{ex_motor}. UPF compensation and complete design for an induction motor load

A three-phase three-wire DSTATCOM at 415 V, 50 Hz provides reactive power compensation for unity power factor to a delta-connected 25 kW induction motor running at 0.8 lagging power factor. Calculate (a) the supply line current before and after compensation, (b) the equivalent per-phase resistance of the compensated load, (c) the DSTATCOM current, (d) its kVA rating, (e) the interfacing inductance and (f) the DC-bus capacitance. Use $f_s=2.5$ kHz, $V_{dc}=700$ V held within 8 %, ripple current 15 % of the DSTATCOM current, $m=1$, $a=1.2$, $k=1$ and a recovery time of 10 ms.

**Solution.** $V_{ph}=415/\sqrt3=239.6$ V. (a) Before compensation $I_s=25\,000/(\sqrt3\times415\times0.8)=43.48$ A. After compensation only the active component remains: $I_s=43.48\times0.8=34.78$ A. (b) $R_{eq}=239.6/34.78=6.89\ \Omega$ per phase (star equivalent). (c) $I_{DST}=43.48\times0.6=26.09$ A. (d) $S_{DST}=3\times239.6\times26.09=18.75$ kVA, equal to the motor's reactive power $25\tan36.87^\circ=18.75$ kvar. (e) $\Delta i_{cr}=0.15\times26.09=3.91$ A, so from EQ{lr}, $L_r=\sqrt3\times700/(12\times1.2\times2500\times3.91)=8.61$ mH. (f) With an 8 % band, $V_{dc1}=644$ V. From EQ{cdc} with $a=1.2$, $\Delta E=3\times239.6\times(1.2\times26.09)\times0.01=225.0$ J and $C_{dc}=2\times225.0/(700^2-644^2)=5.98$ mF. Without the overload factor ($a=1$) the value would be 4.98 mF, so a design must state which convention it uses. The switch ratings from EQ{isw} are $I_{sw}=1.25(3.91+\sqrt2\times26.09)=51.0$ A and $V_{sw}=770$ V, so 1200 V, 75 A devices are a reasonable choice. A ripple filter with $R_f=5\ \Omega$ and corner frequency $f_s/2=1.25$ kHz needs $C_f=25.5\ \mu$F.

#### EXDEF{ex_balance}. Balancing a single-phase load connected across two lines

A three-phase three-wire DSTATCOM at 415 V, 50 Hz is used for load balancing and power-factor correction of a single-phase 50 kW unity-power-factor load connected between lines a and b. Calculate (a) the load line currents, (b) the supply line currents after compensation, (c) the DSTATCOM currents and (d) its kVA rating.

**Solution.** (a) $V_{ab}=415\angle30^\circ$ V and $|Z_L|=415^2/50\,000=3.44\ \Omega$, so $I_{La}=120.48\angle30^\circ$ A, $I_{Lb}=-I_{La}=120.48\angle-150^\circ$ A and $I_{Lc}=0$. (b) The balanced UPF source currents carry 50 kW: $I_s=50\,000/(3\times239.6)=69.56$ A, so $I_{sa}=69.56\angle0^\circ$, $I_{sb}=69.56\angle-120^\circ$ and $I_{sc}=69.56\angle120^\circ$ A. (c) From $I_c=I_L-I_s$: $I_{ca}=69.56\angle60^\circ$ A, $I_{cb}=69.56\angle180^\circ$ A and $I_{cc}=69.56\angle-60^\circ$ A. (d) $S_{DST}=239.6\times3\times69.56=50.0$ kVA. The DSTATCOM rating equals the load rating. This is a general result for a single-phase resistive load balanced by an ideal compensator: the negative-sequence power to be circulated equals the load power, the same conclusion reached for the passive Steinmetz circuit, now realized with a converter.

#### EXDEF{ex_4w}. Four-wire unbalanced star load realized as a balanced UPF load

A three-phase four-wire unbalanced star-connected load with $Z_a=9.0+j3.0$ pu, $Z_b=3.0+j1.5$ pu and $Z_c=7.5+j1.5$ pu (base impedance $9.15\ \Omega$) is supplied at 415 V, 50 Hz. A four-leg DSTATCOM realizes it as a balanced UPF load. Find (a) the source currents, (b) the DSTATCOM phase currents, (c) its neutral current, (d) its kVA rating and (e) the equivalent per-phase resistance.

**Solution.** In ohms, $Z_a=82.35+j27.45$, $Z_b=27.45+j13.73$ and $Z_c=68.63+j13.73\ \Omega$. The load currents are $I_{La}=2.760\angle-18.43^\circ$, $I_{Lb}=7.807\angle-146.57^\circ$ and $I_{Lc}=3.424\angle108.69^\circ$ A. Total real power is $\sum|I_{Lk}|^2R_k=3104.9$ W, or 1035.0 W per phase. (a) $I_s=1035.0/239.6=4.32$ A in each phase, in phase with its voltage. (b) $I_{ca}=1.912\angle-152.84^\circ$, $I_{cb}=4.391\angle-172.66^\circ$ and $I_{cc}=1.173\angle-25.10^\circ$ A. (c) The load neutral current is $I_{Ln}=\sum I_{Lk}=5.354\angle-158.85^\circ$ A; the DSTATCOM neutral leg carries the same current, so the source neutral carries none. (d) Summing phase-leg VA, $S_{DST}=239.6\times(1.912+4.391+1.173)=1.79$ kVA, with the neutral leg rated for 5.35 A. (e) $R_{eq}=239.6/4.32=55.5\ \Omega$. The DSTATCOM supplies the load's reactive power (1.21 kvar in total) and all of its negative- and zero-sequence current, yet its rating is only 1.79 kVA because these duties partly cancel in each leg. A neutral-leg current should not be multiplied by a phase voltage when rating the converter, because the neutral leg's AC terminal is at neutral potential.

#### EXDEF{ex_zvr}. Load balancing and zero voltage regulation on a weak feeder

A 415 V, 50 Hz supply has a feeder impedance of $1+j3\ \Omega$ per phase. A single-phase load $Z_a=10+j5\ \Omega$ is connected between phase a and neutral; phases b and c are unloaded. A four-leg DSTATCOM at the load bus balances the load and regulates the load-bus voltage to 239.6 V per phase. Find (a) the load-bus voltage without compensation, (b) the source currents, (c) the DSTATCOM currents and (d) its rating.

**Solution.** (a) Without compensation, phase a sees $V_{La}=239.6Z_a/(Z_a+Z_s)=196.9\angle-9.46^\circ$ V, an 18 % sag in that phase. (b) With $V_L=239.6$ V, $I_{La}=239.6/(10+j5)=21.43\angle-26.57^\circ$ A and $P_L=21.43^2\times10=4592.7$ W, or 1530.9 W per phase, so $I_p=6.389$ A. In EQ{zvr}, $A=245.99$ and $B=19.17$, giving $10I_q^2-1437.6I_q+3470.0=0$ and $I_q=2.456$ A. Thus $I_{sa}=6.389+j2.456=6.845\angle21.02^\circ$ A, and $I_{sb}$, $I_{sc}$ have the same magnitude displaced by $\mp120^\circ$. The source EMF then leads the load bus by $5.18^\circ$. Because the source current leads the bus voltage, 588.4 var per phase flows from the load bus back toward the source, and this reactive flow through $X_s$ is what lifts the bus voltage. (c) $I_{ca}=I_{La}-I_{sa}=17.56\angle-43.29^\circ$ A, $I_{cb}=-I_{sb}=6.845\angle81.02^\circ$ A and $I_{cc}=-I_{sc}=6.845\angle-38.98^\circ$ A. The neutral leg carries $21.43\angle-26.57^\circ$ A, the whole load current returning through the compensator. (d) $S_{DST}=239.6\times(17.56+6.845+6.845)=7.49$ kVA, plus a 21.4 A neutral leg. The feeder drop is $|Z_sI_s|=21.6$ V, but because the source current leads, the drop is almost perpendicular to the load voltage and $|V_s|=|V_L|$: the ZVR principle of FIG{dst_phasor}(c).

#### EXDEF{ex_pfc}. Power-factor correction rating

A 415 V, 50 Hz system feeds a 45 kW star-connected load at 0.72 lagging power factor. Find the source current before and after UPF compensation, the load reactive power, the DSTATCOM current and kVA, and the equivalent resistance after compensation.

**Solution.** Before: $I_s=45\,000/(\sqrt3\times415\times0.72)=86.95$ A. After: $I_s=45\,000/(\sqrt3\times415)=62.60$ A, a 28 % reduction. $Q_L=45\tan(\cos^{-1}0.72)=43.37$ kvar. $I_{DST}=43\,373/(\sqrt3\times415)=60.34$ A and $S_{DST}=43.37$ kVA. $R_{eq}=239.6/62.60=3.83\ \Omega$ per phase.

#### EXDEF{ex_lr}. Interfacing inductor

A DSTATCOM operates with $V_{dc}=900$ V, $f_s=4$ kHz, $m=1$, $a=1.2$ and a permitted ripple of 10 % of its 50 A rated current. Find $L_r$.

**Solution.** $\Delta i_{cr}=5$ A, and EQ{lr} gives $L_r=\sqrt3\times900/(12\times1.2\times4000\times5)=5.41$ mH.

#### EXDEF{ex_harm}. Harmonic compensation current and rating

A 415 V nonlinear load draws 40 A fundamental (in phase with the voltage) with 5th and 7th harmonics of 20 % and 15 % of the fundamental. Find the RMS load current, the harmonic current, the DSTATCOM current for sinusoidal source current and its rating.

**Solution.** $I_L=40\sqrt{1+0.2^2+0.15^2}=41.23$ A and $I_h=40\sqrt{0.2^2+0.15^2}=10.0$ A, so the current THD is 25 %. The true power factor of the load is $1/\sqrt{1+0.25^2}=0.970$ from EQ{pf}, even though its displacement factor is 1; a capacitor bank could not improve it.

#### EXDEF{ex_multi}. Rating for several loads

A 415 V system supplies 30 kW at 0.8 lagging and 20 kW at 0.9 lagging. Find the DSTATCOM rating for UPF and the source current after compensation.

**Solution.** $P=50$ kW and $Q=30\times0.75+20\times0.4843=32.19$ kvar, so $S_{DST}=32.19$ kVA. After compensation $I_s=50\,000/(\sqrt3\times415)=69.56$ A.

#### EXDEF{ex_clarke}. Clarke transform verification

With balanced voltages $v_a=V_m\cos\omega t$, $v_b=V_m\cos(\omega t-120^\circ)$, $v_c=V_m\cos(\omega t+120^\circ)$, a balanced linear load draws $i_a=10\cos\omega t$, $i_b=10\cos(\omega t-120^\circ)$, $i_c=10\cos(\omega t+120^\circ)$ A. Using EQ{clarke}, find $i_\alpha$ and $i_\beta$ at $\omega t=0$ and the instantaneous powers.

**Solution.** At $\omega t=0$: $i_a=10$ and $i_b=i_c=-5$ A, so $i_\alpha=(2/3)(10+2.5+2.5)=10$ A and $i_\beta=0$. Throughout the cycle $i_\alpha=10\cos\omega t$ and $i_\beta=10\sin\omega t$. With the stated in-phase voltages, $p=10V_m$, $q=0$ and $P_{3\phi}=15V_m$. Current data alone, without a voltage reference, could not establish zero reactive power: a circular current locus shows balanced sinusoidal current, not unity power factor.

#### EXDEF{ex_pq}. Complete three-phase pq compensation calculation

An ideal balanced three-wire 50 Hz supply has $v_k=100\cos\theta_k$ V with $\theta_a=\theta$, $\theta_b=\theta-2\pi/3$, $\theta_c=\theta+2\pi/3$. The load currents are $i_{L,k}=10\cos(\theta_k-\pi/6)+2\cos5\theta_k+\cos7\theta_k$ A (peak values). Derive the shunt current reference that cancels harmonics and fundamental reactive current, evaluate it at $\theta=\pi/6$ and check the source current and powers.

**Solution.** The fifth harmonic is negative sequence and the seventh positive sequence for these phase shifts. The Clarke transform gives $v_\alpha=100\cos\theta$, $v_\beta=100\sin\theta$, $i_{L,\alpha}=10\cos(\theta-\pi/6)+2\cos5\theta+\cos7\theta$ and $i_{L,\beta}=10\sin(\theta-\pi/6)-2\sin5\theta+\sin7\theta$. From EQ{pq}, $p=866.025+300\cos6\theta$ and $q=-500-100\sin6\theta$, so $\bar p=866.025$, $\bar q=-500$, $\tilde p=300\cos6\theta$ and $\tilde q=-100\sin6\theta$.

Set $p_C=\tilde p$ and $q_C=q$. At $\theta=\pi/6$: $(v_\alpha,v_\beta)=(86.603,50)$ V, $(p_C,q_C)=(-300,-500)$ and $v_\alpha^2+v_\beta^2=10\,000$. EQ{pqinv} gives $i_{C,\alpha}=[86.603(-300)-50(-500)]/10\,000=-0.098$ A and $i_{C,\beta}=[50(-300)+86.603(-500)]/10\,000=-5.830$ A. The inverse Clarke transform gives $(i_{C,a},i_{C,b},i_{C,c})=(-0.098,-5.000,5.098)$ A. The load currents at the same instant are $(7.402,-5.000,-2.402)$ A, so $i_s=i_L-i_C=(7.5,0,-7.5)$ A. Over the whole cycle $i_{s,k}=8.660\cos\theta_k$ A: balanced sinusoids in phase with their voltages, 6.124 A RMS.
