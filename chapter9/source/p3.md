
## 9.6 Series and Hybrid Active Power Filters

Three ideas are often mixed together and should be kept apart. A shunt active filter creates a compensating current; a series active filter creates a compensating voltage; a hybrid filter deliberately assigns part of the harmonic spectrum to passive components so that the converter handles only a residual or only a small voltage. The engineering question is therefore not merely whether harmonics exist, but what kind of source produces them, where they originate and which impedance path the compensator must create. A DSTATCOM operated purely for harmonic cancellation is a shunt active power filter, and its pq and SRF control were developed in Section 9.3.3. This section builds the network-level theory that decides which filter arrangement suits a given load and supply [8], [27], [28], [29], [30].

### 9.6.1 Harmonic-source models

Peng's classification of harmonic sources is the starting point [28] (FIG{hsrc}). A thyristor rectifier with a large DC inductor draws a nearly rectangular current set by the DC-side inductance; seen from the AC side it is a harmonic *current source* $I_{Lh}$ with a large parallel impedance $Z_L$ (Norton form). A diode rectifier with a large DC capacitor, typical of drives, switch-mode supplies and EV chargers, draws a pulsed current whose shape is set by the AC impedance in front of it; seen from the AC side it is a harmonic *voltage source* $V_{Lh}$ behind a small series impedance $Z_L$ (Thevenin form). The supply is represented at each harmonic by a background voltage $V_{sh}$ behind the source impedance $Z_s=R_s+jhX_s$.

![FIGDEF{hsrc} Harmonic-source models: (a) current-source type load (Norton), for which a shunt filter is natural; (b) voltage-source type load (Thevenin), for which a series filter is natural.](fig/f9_hsources.png){width=94%}

The distinction matters because the harmonic current that reaches the source depends on the ratio of the load's own impedance to the impedance the filter presents. A shunt path in parallel with a current-source load diverts the current; the same shunt path in parallel with a voltage-source load lowers the PCC impedance seen by the load and can actually increase the harmonic current it draws. A series impedance in front of a voltage-source load limits that current directly. The rest of the section quantifies these statements.

### 9.6.2 Passive and shunt active filtering of a current-source load

For a current-source load with a shunt passive filter of harmonic impedance $Z_F$ at the PCC, superposition at harmonic $h$ gives the source current

$$I_{sh}=\frac{Z_F}{Z_s+Z_F}\,I_{Lh}+\frac{1}{Z_s+Z_F}\,V_{sh}$$ EQDEF{passive}

The filtering characteristic is set by the ratio $Z_F/(Z_s+Z_F)$, so it depends on the source impedance, which the designer does not control and which changes with network configuration. Two failure modes follow directly from EQ{passive}. Below its tuning frequency the filter is capacitive, and it forms a parallel resonance with the inductive source at which $|Z_s+Z_F|$ becomes small: both terms of EQ{passive} are then amplified. And because $Z_F$ is low at its tuning frequency, the filter also sinks background distortion $V_{sh}$ from the rest of the network and can be overloaded by harmonics it was never intended to absorb. For a plain capacitor bank of reactive power $Q_c$ on a bus of short-circuit power $S_{sc}$, the parallel resonance occurs near

$$h_r\approx\sqrt{\frac{S_{sc}}{Q_c}}$$ EQDEF{hres}

and a series detuning reactor of $p$ percent of the capacitor reactance moves the series resonance of the branch to $h_t=1/\sqrt{p}$, below the lowest characteristic harmonic.

An ideal shunt active filter with load-current detection injects $i_c=G\,i_{Lh}$, where $G$ is the harmonic gain of the reference generator ($G=1$ for complete cancellation). In parallel with a current-source load,

$$I_{sh}=(1-G)\frac{Z_L}{Z_s+Z_L}\,I_{Lh}+\frac{1}{Z_s+Z_L}\,V_{sh}$$ EQDEF{shuntapf}

so with $G\to1$ the load harmonics vanish from the source independently of $Z_s$, which is the main advantage over a passive filter. The background term is not compensated in this mode; it is small for a current-source load because $Z_L$ is large. The cost is a converter rated for the full harmonic (and any reactive or unbalanced) current, $S_{APF}=3V_{ph}\sqrt{\sum_hI_{ch}^2}$, which is economical at low voltage and becomes expensive at medium voltage.

### 9.6.3 Series active filter: voltage cancellation and harmonic isolation

A series APF (FIG{apf}(a)) can serve two distinct objectives. For *voltage cancellation* it isolates the load from measured supply-voltage harmonics by commanding $v_{inj,h}=-v_{s,h}$ within its voltage, current and energy limits. The desired fundamental is estimated first: a PLL with positive-sequence extraction and filtering in a synchronous frame gives $v_{s,1}^{+}$, and the reference becomes $v_{inj}^{*}=v_{s,1}^{+}-v_s$. With a balanced fundamental supply the residue contains only harmonics; with an unbalanced supply it also contains unwanted sequence components and must not be called harmonics alone. More generally, any desired load waveform $v_L^{*}$ gives $v_{inj}^{*}=v_L^{*}-v_s$, consistent with EQ{dvrkvl}, which is why the DVR and the series APF are the same hardware with different references.

![FIGDEF{apf} (a) Series active filter with a shunt passive filter; (b) hybrid shunt filter with the active stage in series with a tuned LC branch.](fig/image42.png){width=90%}

For *harmonic isolation* the series converter synthesizes a resistance at harmonic frequencies only. Its reference is derived from the detected harmonic source current, $v_c=K\,i_{sh}$ in the voltage-drop sense, with $K$ in ohms (or per unit) and zero gain at the fundamental, so the converter presents a high series impedance to harmonics and none to the fundamental. Combined with a shunt passive filter, as proposed by Peng, Akagi and Nabae [27], the source harmonic current becomes

$$I_{sh}=\frac{Z_F}{Z_s+Z_F+K}\,I_{Lh}+\frac{1}{Z_s+Z_F+K}\,V_{sh}$$ EQDEF{serieshybrid}

When $K\gg|Z_s+Z_F|$, both terms are suppressed: the filtering no longer depends on $Z_s$, the passive filter no longer sinks background distortion from the network, and the parallel resonance between $Z_F$ and $Z_s$ is damped because $K$ acts as a resistor inside the resonant loop. FIG{hyb_resp} shows the effect for a 5th-harmonic tuned branch. The series converter carries the full line current, but its voltage is only $V_{ch}=K I_{sh}$, which is small once the harmonics have been isolated, so its kVA rating is typically a few percent of the load rating. A series filter alone cannot remove a nonlinear load's harmonic current, which needs a local shunt path; the passive branch provides it.

![FIGDEF{hyb_resp} Frequency response of a shunt passive filter (tuned near the 5th, $Q=30$) on a source of $X_s=0.06$ pu, with and without a series active filter of harmonic gain $K$: (a) fraction of load harmonic current reaching the source; (b) source harmonic current per unit background voltage.](fig/f9_hybrid_resp.png){width=94%}

For a voltage-source type load the series filter plays a different role. The load harmonic current is $I_{Lh}=(V_{Lh}-V_{pcc,h})/Z_L$ with small $Z_L$, so a series impedance $K$ in front of the load gives $I_{sh}=(V_{Lh}-V_{sh})/(Z_s+Z_L+K)$, which falls with $K$ regardless of the source. A shunt active filter on such a load works against a low impedance and must inject a larger current than the load originally drew; this is the physical reason Peng recommends series compensation for capacitive-smoothed rectifiers and shunt compensation for inductive-smoothed ones [28].

### 9.6.4 Hybrid shunt filter with the active stage in series with an LC branch

A full shunt APF must carry its whole compensating current, and a series APF must carry the line current even when its voltage is small. In the hybrid arrangement of FIG{apf}(b), due to Fujita and Akagi [29], the active stage is placed in series with a tuned LC branch. Most of the fundamental voltage appears across the branch capacitor, so the converter needs only a small voltage to sharpen the branch's harmonic impedance and damp its resonance with the supply. If the converter is controlled as $v_{AF}=K\,i_{sh}$, the branch behaves as $Z_F+K$ in the harmonic loop and EQ{serieshybrid} applies with the same benefits. The converter carries the branch current, including its fundamental capacitive component $I_{F1}\approx V_{ph}/X_{F1}$ and all its harmonic components, not the residual source current:

$$S_{AF}=V_{AF,rms}\,I_{F,rms},\qquad I_{F,rms}=\sqrt{I_{F1}^{2}+\sum_{h}I_{Fh}^{2}},\qquad V_{AF,rms}\approx K\sqrt{\sum_hI_{sh}^{2}}$$ EQDEF{saf}

Because $V_{AF}$ is a few percent of the supply voltage, the converter rating is a few percent of the load kVA even though its current is large. Other hybrids place a small shunt active filter in parallel with a passive filter; then the active stage carries only the residual. The two are easily confused, and Example EX{ex_hyb_branch} shows the consequence of doing so.

#### EXDEF{ex_resonance}. Capacitor-bank resonance and detuning

An 11 kV bus has a short-circuit level of 100 MVA. A 3 Mvar power-factor-correction bank is to be added near a large six-pulse drive. Find the parallel resonant order and the order to which a 7 % detuning reactor tunes the branch.

**Solution.** From EQ{hres}, $h_r=\sqrt{100/3}=5.77$, uncomfortably close to the 5th harmonic produced by the drive; the bank would amplify it. A 7 % series reactor tunes the branch to $h_t=1/\sqrt{0.07}=3.78$, below the 5th, so the branch is inductive at all characteristic harmonics and the resonance with the source moves below $h=3.8$. A frequency scan over all credible network configurations is still required, because $S_{sc}$ changes with switching state.

#### EXDEF{ex_serhyb}. Series active filter with a shunt passive filter

At the 5th harmonic a supply has $Z_s=0.02+j0.30$ pu and a shunt passive filter, slightly detuned by ageing, has $Z_F=0.02+j0.05$ pu. The load injects $I_{L5}=0.20$ pu and the background supply distortion is $V_{s5}=0.01$ pu. Find the source 5th-harmonic current with the passive filter alone and with a series active filter of $K=1$ pu, and estimate the series-filter voltage and kVA on the load base.

**Solution.** Passive only, from EQ{passive}: $|Z_F/(Z_s+Z_F)|=0.0539/0.3506=0.153$, so the load contributes $0.031$ pu, and the background contributes $0.01/0.3506=0.028$ pu. Depending on phase, the source 5th-harmonic current is up to $0.059$ pu, and the filter is also absorbing distortion from the rest of the network. With $K=1$ pu, EQ{serieshybrid} gives $0.0539/1.078\times0.20=0.0098$ pu and $0.01/1.078=0.0093$ pu, at most $0.019$ pu in total, a reduction of about two thirds even with the filter detuned. The series-filter voltage is $V_{c5}=K I_{s5}\le0.019$ pu. Since it carries the full 1 pu line current, its rating is about $0.019\times1.0=1.9$ % of the load kVA, compared with roughly 20 % for a shunt APF that would have to carry the full 0.20 pu harmonic current.

### 9.6.5 Choosing among filter arrangements

A shunt APF and a DSTATCOM share the same hardware; the difference is the control objective. An APF emphasizes harmonic and unbalanced-current cancellation, while a DSTATCOM often includes fundamental reactive power and voltage regulation, and one converter can do both within its RMS current and bandwidth. TAB{filters} collects the trade-offs derived above.

## 9.7 IEEE Std 519-2022 Compliance at the Distribution Level

IEEE Std 519-2022 [9] is a system-level standard applied at the point of common coupling (PCC), not a statement that a particular converter has "good THD". Its philosophy is a shared responsibility: the user limits the harmonic current injected at the PCC, and the system owner limits the voltage distortion delivered there. Current compliance is judged by total demand distortion and by individual harmonics referred to the maximum demand current, with limits that depend on the short-circuit ratio; voltage limits are checked separately. A device-level current THD measured at one operating point therefore cannot by itself establish compliance.

### 9.7.1 Definitions and the TDD-THD relation

The PCC is the point on the public system electrically nearest to the user's installation where other customers are, or could be, connected. For harmonic currents $I_h$ at the PCC,

$$\mathrm{TDD}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{h=2}^{50}I_h^{2}}}{I_L}\times100\ \%$$ EQDEF{tdd}

where $I_L$ is the maximum demand load current, established as the average of the maximum demand currents (15- or 30-minute demand) over the preceding twelve months. It is not the instantaneous load current used elsewhere in this chapter. The voltage THD at the PCC is

$$\mathrm{THD}_V=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{h=2}^{50}V_h^{2}}}{V_1}\times100\ \%$$ EQDEF{thdv}

THD uses the contemporaneous fundamental $I_1$, whereas TDD uses the fixed $I_L$, so

$$\mathrm{TDD}=\mathrm{THD}_I\,\frac{I_1}{I_L}$$ EQDEF{tddthd}

A lightly loaded drive can show a very high current THD while its TDD is small, because its harmonic currents are small in amperes (FIG{tdd_thd}). This is exactly why the standard uses TDD: it penalizes harmonic amperes, which create voltage distortion, rather than distortion percentages at light load.

![FIGDEF{tdd_thd} TDD versus loading for several values of current THD, with the 5 %, 8 % and 12 % TDD limits for $I_{sc}/I_L<20$, 20 to 50 and 50 to 100.](fig/f9_tdd_thd.png){width=70%}

### 9.7.3 Why current limits scale with the short-circuit ratio

The structure of TAB{ieee519i} follows from the voltage drop that a harmonic current produces. With the source treated as inductive at the PCC, the fundamental short-circuit reactance is $X_1=V_1/I_{sc}$ per phase, so the harmonic reactance is $hX_1$ and

$$\frac{V_h}{V_1}=\frac{h\,X_1I_h}{V_1}=h\,\frac{I_h}{I_{sc}}=\frac{h}{I_{sc}/I_L}\cdot\frac{I_h}{I_L}$$ EQDEF{vhih}

An installation on a stiff bus (large $I_{sc}/I_L$) can inject more current for the same voltage distortion, which is why the permitted percentages rise with the short-circuit ratio. For example, at $I_{sc}/I_L=20$ the 4 % limit on the 5th harmonic produces $V_5/V_1=5\times0.04/20=1$ %, comfortably inside the 3 % individual voltage limit at medium voltage, so that several users can share the voltage headroom. EQ{vhih} ignores network resonance, background distortion and capacitor banks, all of which a real study must include.

### 9.7.4 Statistical assessment

The limits are applied statistically, using the aggregation intervals of IEC 61000-4-30 [32]: very-short-time values over 3 s and short-time values over 10 min, each obtained as the RMS of the shorter-interval values,

$$X_{10\,min}=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{3\,s,k}^{2}}$$ EQDEF{aggr}

For current, the daily 99th-percentile very-short-time values should be below twice the table values, the weekly 99th-percentile short-time values below 1.5 times the table values, and the weekly 95th-percentile short-time values below the table values. For voltage, the daily 99th-percentile very-short-time values should be below 1.5 times the table values and the weekly 95th-percentile short-time values below them. FIG{stat} illustrates the procedure for a synthetic week of 10-min TDD values: occasional values exceed 5 %, but the 95th percentile (4.73 %) is below the limit and the 99th percentile (5.19 %) is below 1.5 times the limit, so this record passes the short-time tests.

![FIGDEF{stat} Illustrative statistical assessment of one week of 10-min TDD values ($I_{sc}/I_L<20$): (a) time record; (b) cumulative distribution with the 95th and 99th percentiles. Synthetic data for explanation only.](fig/f9_stat.png){width=94%}

### 9.7.5 Several harmonic sources at one PCC

When several nonlinear loads share a PCC, their harmonic currents add as phasors, and their phase angles become increasingly random at higher orders. IEC TR 61000-3-6 [31] uses a general summation law,

$$I_h=\left(\sum_iI_{h,i}^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha},\qquad \alpha=\begin{cases}1,&h<5\\ 1.4,&5\le h\le10\\ 2,&h>10\end{cases}$$ EQDEF{summation}

which lies between arithmetic addition ($\alpha=1$, worst case, appropriate for low orders where loads are in phase) and RMS addition ($\alpha=2$, random phase). Using arithmetic addition at all orders overstates the higher-order harmonics and can lead to an oversized filter.

### 9.7.6 Sizing mitigation for compliance

A compensator is sized from the residual spectrum it must leave. If a shunt APF removes a fraction $(1-k)$ of every harmonic uniformly, the residual is $kI_h$ and the required harmonic current rating is

$$I_{APF}=(1-k)\sqrt{\sum_hI_h^{2}},\qquad k=\min\left(\frac{\mathrm{TDD}_{target}}{\mathrm{TDD}},\ \min_h\frac{I_{h,lim}}{I_h}\right)$$ EQDEF{apfsize}

A selective-harmonic design can do better by attenuating only the offending orders, at the price of a more complex controller. FIG{workflow} summarizes the compliance workflow. Interharmonics, rapidly varying distortion and transients need separate interpretation, because the standard is framed around steady-state limits.

#### EXDEF{ex_tddthd}. THD versus TDD at light load

A drive operating at 40 % of the plant's maximum demand current draws a current with 20 % THD. Find its TDD.

**Solution.** From EQ{tddthd}, $\mathrm{TDD}=20\times0.40=8$ %. The same harmonic amperes at full load would give 20 % THD and 20 % TDD, while at 40 % load they read as 20 % THD but only 8 % TDD. Quoting THD at light load overstates the network impact.

#### EXDEF{ex_vdist}. Voltage distortion produced by a harmonic current spectrum

The installation of Example EX{ex_tdd} ($I_{sc}=14$ kA; $I_5=14$, $I_7=9$, $I_{11}=5$, $I_{13}=3.5$, $I_{17}=2$ A) is connected at a medium-voltage PCC. Estimate the voltage distortion it causes, ignoring resonance and background distortion.

**Solution.** From EQ{vhih}, $V_5/V_1=5\times14/14\,000=0.50$ %, $V_7/V_1=0.45$ %, $V_{11}/V_1=0.39$ %, $V_{13}/V_1=0.33$ % and $V_{17}/V_1=0.24$ %, so $\mathrm{THD}_V=0.88$ %. Every value is well inside the 3 % individual and 5 % total limits for $1<V\le69$ kV. The installation's own contribution is small; any compliance problem at this PCC would come from background distortion or resonance, which a frequency scan must check.

#### EXDEF{ex_apf519}. Sizing a shunt APF for compliance

A 415 V installation has $I_L=500$ A and $I_{sc}=8$ kA. Its spectrum is $I_5=40$, $I_7=25$, $I_{11}=12$, $I_{13}=8$, $I_{17}=5$ and $I_{19}=4$ A. Check compliance and size a shunt APF that brings the TDD to 4 % with uniform attenuation.

**Solution.** $I_{sc}/I_L=16<20$, so the limits are 4 % (20 A) for $h<11$, 2 % (10 A) for $11\le h<17$, 1.5 % (7.5 A) for $17\le h<23$, and 5 % TDD. The spectrum is 8.0, 5.0, 2.4, 1.6, 1.0 and 0.8 % of $I_L$: the 5th, 7th and 11th exceed their limits, and $\sqrt{\sum I_h^2}=49.74$ A gives TDD = 9.95 %. Cutting each harmonic only to its individual limit would leave $\sqrt{20^2+20^2+10^2+8^2+5^2+4^2}=31.7$ A, or 6.34 % TDD, still failing, so the TDD governs. From EQ{apfsize}, $k=4/9.95=0.402$; the residual spectrum (16.1, 10.1, 4.8, 3.2, 2.0 and 1.6 A) meets every limit (FIG{spectrum}). The APF must supply $I_{APF}=(1-0.402)\times49.74=29.7$ A RMS of harmonic current, or $3\times239.6\times29.7=21.4$ kVA, before allowances for reactive power, ripple and overload.

#### EXDEF{ex_sum}. Summation of two drives

Two drives at one PCC produce 5th-harmonic currents of 30 A and 20 A and 11th-harmonic currents of 8 A and 6 A. Estimate the combined values with EQ{summation}.

**Solution.** For $h=5$, $\alpha=1.4$: $(30^{1.4}+20^{1.4})^{1/1.4}=41.3$ A, between the arithmetic sum of 50 A and the RMS sum of 36.1 A. For $h=11$, $\alpha=2$: $\sqrt{8^2+6^2}=10.0$ A, against an arithmetic 14 A. Arithmetic addition would oversize a filter for the 11th harmonic by 40 %.

## 9.8 Comparing and Selecting Custom Power Devices

Device selection should begin with the disturbed state variable and the required continuity of service. A voltage-quality problem upstream of a sensitive load points first toward a series compensator; a nonlinear or unbalanced load that pollutes the source points toward a shunt compensator; simultaneous voltage and current problems motivate a UPQC; a true interruption needs an energy-supported island or UPS function rather than a conventional conditioner. Converter rating, energy duration, fault duty and bypass philosophy are checked next, and only then is a control algorithm chosen. This section turns those statements into quantitative screening tools.

### 9.8.1 Functional comparison

TAB{dev_cmp} compares the three main devices by function, and FIG{map} arranges the candidate devices by the kind of disturbance and the side on which it originates. A DSTATCOM can move voltage through the feeder impedance but cannot guarantee restoration of a deep, externally imposed sag or an interruption. A DVR can compensate supply-voltage harmonics if designed for the bandwidth. A UPQC combines both services, subject to both converters' limits, the DC balance and the topology. The map is a first screen only: a DVR may suffice for a semiconductor process dominated by short sags, whereas the same plant may need a UPQC if its own drives also cause unacceptable source-current distortion; conversely, a UPQC where only displacement power factor is poor is unjustified if a capacitor bank or DSTATCOM meets the dynamic requirement.

| Main problems solved | Reactive power, unbalance, neutral current, current harmonics, flicker | Sag, swell, voltage unbalance, voltage harmonics, notches | All of these |

### 9.8.2 Interruptions are an energy problem

A sag leaves some source voltage, so a grid-fed UPQC or a rectifier-supported DVR can draw the missing power from the remaining supply. An interruption leaves none. The protected load must then be fed entirely from storage, through a topology that isolates it from the dead network and forms its voltage and frequency. With usable stored energy $E_{us}$ and load power $P_L$, the ideal ride-through time is

$$t_{rt}=\frac{\eta\,E_{us}}{P_L}$$ EQDEF{ridethrough}

which for DC-link capacitors of practical size is a few milliseconds to a few hundred milliseconds. Problem 9.13 shows the scale. Longer interruptions require batteries, flywheels, a standby generator or a UPS, and once isolation and grid-forming control are added, the device is functionally a UPS rather than a power-quality conditioner.

### 9.8.3 Normalized converter ratings

The derivations of Sections 9.3 to 9.5 give closed-form ratings that make devices comparable on one axis. For a load of rated kVA $S_L$, power factor $\cos\phi$, current THD $\mathrm{THD}_I$ and negative-sequence current fraction $u$, full current compensation needs

$$\frac{S_{DST}}{S_L}=\sqrt{\sin^{2}\phi+u^{2}+\mathrm{THD}_I^{2}}$$ EQDEF{sdstn}

An in-phase DVR designed for sag depth $X$ needs $S_{DVR}/S_L=X$ plus allowances for unbalance and phase jump, and a stored energy $E=XP_Lt_{sag}/\eta$. A right-shunt UPQC-P needs, from EQ{ssep} and EQ{ishp} with the harmonic duty added,

$$\frac{S_{UPQC}}{S_L}=\frac{X\cos\phi}{1-X}+\sqrt{\sin^{2}\phi+\mathrm{THD}_I^{2}+\cos^{2}\phi\left(\frac{X}{1-X}\right)^{2}}$$ EQDEF{supqcn}

FIG{rating} plots these ratings for a 0.8 pf load with 25 % current THD. Two conclusions are useful for selection. First, a UPQC-P has roughly the same total converter rating as a separate DVR and DSTATCOM up to sags of about 25 %, but it needs no energy store, because the sag power circulates through the shunt converter. Second, beyond about 30 % the $1/(1-X)$ term makes the UPQC rating rise steeply, and a storage-supported DVR plus a DSTATCOM becomes the leaner combination.

For a DVR the energy per event is $E=XP_Lt_{sag}/\eta$ (EQ{energy}). ### 9.8.5 Economic evaluation

A custom-power device is justified when the losses it avoids exceed its life-cycle cost. The expected annual cost of power-quality events without mitigation is

$$C_{PQ}=\sum_iN_i\,p_{trip,i}\,C_{trip,i}$$ EQDEF{cpq}

where $N_i$ is the annual number of events of class $i$ (from the audit), $p_{trip,i}$ the probability that such an event trips the process (from equipment tolerance, as in IEEE Std 1346 [33]) and $C_{trip,i}$ the cost of one trip, including scrap, restart and lost production. The device's annualized cost is

$$C_{ann}=\mathrm{CRF}\cdot C_{cap}+C_{OM}+C_{loss},\qquad \mathrm{CRF}=\frac{r(1+r)^{n}}{(1+r)^{n}-1}$$ EQDEF{cann}

with capital cost $C_{cap}$, discount rate $r$, life $n$ years, operation and maintenance cost $C_{OM}$ and the cost of converter losses $C_{loss}$. A DVR with bypass in standby has small losses; a DSTATCOM or UPQC that runs continuously can lose 1 to 3 % of its rating, and over a year this term can rival the capital charge. The device pays when $C_{PQ}-C_{PQ,res}>C_{ann}$, where $C_{PQ,res}$ is the residual cost of events the device cannot cover (FIG{econ}).

### 9.8.6 Screening procedure and multi-criteria scoring

FIG{screen} gives a first screening procedure; it is not a procurement rule. The essential first step is a power-quality audit that measures sag depth, duration and phase jumps, separates supply-voltage harmonics from load-current harmonics, checks TDD with the full method of Section 9.7 and identifies interruption exposure, which needs a separate energy path. When more than one device survives the screen, a weighted score $S_j=\sum_kw_kr_{jk}$, with weights $w_k$ agreed in advance and ratings $r_{jk}$ for each candidate $j$ on criteria such as technical coverage, converter rating, energy, losses, footprint, redundancy and cost, makes the trade-off explicit and auditable (Example EX{ex_score}).

#### EXDEF{ex_norm}. Normalized ratings of competing solutions

A 500 kVA, 0.8 pf plant has 25 % current THD and must ride through 30 % balanced sags lasting 300 ms. Compare the converter ratings and storage of (a) a DSTATCOM plus an in-phase DVR and (b) a right-shunt UPQC-P.

**Solution.** (a) From EQ{sdstn} with $u=0$: $S_{DST}/S_L=\sqrt{0.36+0.0625}=0.65$, so 325 kVA. The DVR needs $0.30\times500=150$ kVA plus allowances, and stores $E=0.3\times400\times0.3=36$ kJ per event, so about 475 kVA of converters plus 36 kJ. (b) From EQ{supqcn}: series $0.3\times0.8/0.7=0.343$ and shunt $\sqrt{0.36+0.0625+0.1176}=0.735$, total 1.078, or 539 kVA with no energy store. The UPQC needs about 13 % more converter rating but no storage, while the separate solution needs storage that must be maintained and replaced. Footprint, losses, redundancy and the cost of the store decide between them.

#### EXDEF{ex_econ}. Economic justification of a DVR

A plant experiences 20 exposing sags per year. Without mitigation 60 % of them trip the process at 40 k\$ per trip. A DVR costing 300 k\$ (life 15 years, discount rate 8 %, O&M 6 k\$ per year, negligible standby losses) reduces the trip probability to 5 %. Is it justified?

**Solution.** $C_{PQ}=20\times0.6\times40=480$ k\$ per year; the residual is $20\times0.05\times40=40$ k\$. From EQ{cann}, $\mathrm{CRF}=0.08\times1.08^{15}/(1.08^{15}-1)=0.1168$, so $C_{ann}=0.1168\times300+6=41.0$ k\$ per year. The net annual benefit is $480-40-41=399$ k\$, and the simple payback is $300/(480-40-6)=0.69$ year. The break-even exposure is $41.0/(0.55\times40)=1.9$ events per year (FIG{econ}). The result is driven by the trip cost and the audit's event count, which is why both must be measured rather than assumed.

#### EXDEF{ex_score}. Multi-criteria screening

A semiconductor plant suffers frequent utility-side sags and significant harmonic distortion from its own drives, with comparable production losses from each. A second plant has rare, shallow sags but continuous harmonic distortion well above its TDD limit. Screen the technology for each, and set up a weighted score for the first plant.

**Solution.** For the first plant both problems are present and significant, so a UPQC deserves serious consideration: its shared DC link lets the series converter's sag energy come from the grid through the shunt converter rather than from a separately sized store. With weights of 0.35 (technical coverage), 0.20 (converter rating), 0.15 (storage), 0.15 (losses and footprint) and 0.15 (cost), and ratings from 1 to 5 of 5, 3, 5, 3, 3 for the UPQC and 5, 4, 2, 3, 3 for a DVR plus a shunt APF, the scores are 4.0 and 3.75. The UPQC leads narrowly, so the decision is sensitive to the weights and should be confirmed with the plant's sag statistics, harmonic spectrum, ratings, losses, redundancy and life-cycle cost. For the second plant, a shunt APF or DSTATCOM, or a hybrid filter if a resonance study supports it, is the natural candidate; a DVR is hard to justify on the sag evidence.

## 9.9 Recent Developments and Research Directions

Recent work should be read as an extension of the physical models developed in this chapter. Higher switching frequency changes filter size and loss; grid-forming control changes the converter's synchronization and voltage-source behaviour; predictive and data-assisted control changes how references are computed; digital twins and hardware-in-the-loop change how claims are validated. None of these removes the semiconductor current, DC-energy, transformer, thermal or protection constraints. A recent scoping review of UPQCs in microgrids found that most studies still rely on PI and SRF control and on offline simulation, with comparatively few experimental or long-term stability assessments [24].

### 9.9.1 Wide-bandgap devices and higher switching frequency

SiC and GaN devices allow higher switching frequency and lower switching loss than silicon devices in suitable voltage classes. For custom-power equipment this shrinks the ripple filter (EQ{rf} scales $C_f$ with $1/f_s$) and the interfacing inductor (EQ{lr} scales $L_r$ with $1/f_s$), and it widens the current-loop bandwidth available for harmonic tracking. The gain is not free: $dv/dt$, common-mode current, insulation stress, electromagnetic compatibility, gate-drive design and thermal cycling become more demanding, and at medium voltage series connection or multilevel structures remain necessary. A meaningful comparison uses total converter loss, filter volume, junction-temperature swing and conducted emissions, not switching frequency alone.

### 9.9.2 Grid-forming and weak-grid operation

Conventional custom-power controllers assume a grid stiff enough for a PLL to extract the angle. In converter-dominated or island-capable microgrids this can fail, and the converter can no longer be treated as an ideal current or voltage source behind a fixed impedance. Its closed-loop output impedance $Z_{out}(s)$ interacts with the network impedance $Z_g(s)$, and a screening condition is based on the minor-loop ratio

$$L(s)=\frac{Z_g(s)}{Z_{out}(s)}$$ EQDEF{minor}

with a generalized Nyquist assessment required for coupled dq or sequence-domain models. Current limiting is critical because a grid-forming voltage controller may demand currents far beyond semiconductor capability during faults or deep sags. Grid codes now specify explicit tests of grid-forming functions, including voltage-source behaviour, fast fault-current injection and acting as a sink for harmonics and unbalance, rather than relying on nominal small-signal performance [25], [26]. For a DSTATCOM or UPQC, the research question is how voltage-source behaviour coexists with current limits and with the fast harmonic-compensation loops. Interconnection requirements for distributed energy resources, such as those of IEEE Std 1547-2018 [21], constrain what a converter sharing a DC link with generation may do during disturbances.

### 9.9.3 Predictive, repetitive, adaptive and data-assisted control

Model-predictive control can handle multivariable converter constraints explicitly. A generic constrained formulation for a UPQC is

$$J=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(w_v\left\Vert v_L-v_L^{*}\right\Vert^{2}+w_i\left\Vert i_s-i_s^{*}\right\Vert^{2}+w_{dc}\left(V_{dc}-V_{dc}^{*}\right)^{2}+w_u\left\Vert\Delta u\right\Vert^{2}\right)$$ EQDEF{mpc}

subject to converter voltage, current, DC-energy and switching constraints. Repetitive and resonant controllers target periodic distortion, and adaptive observers or data-driven classifiers can improve disturbance identification or tune references. These methods should be evaluated against conventional SRF or PR control using the same converter limits, sampling delay, sensor noise and grid-impedance uncertainty, and the report should include execution time and robustness to parameter error. A lower simulation THD alone is not evidence of a better practical controller.

### 9.9.4 Digital twins, HIL and protection-aware validation

A digital twin of a UPQC or DVR is more than a simulation model. At minimum it synchronizes measured converter states with an electrical model, estimates slowly varying parameters such as filter inductance or DC-capacitor condition, compares predicted and measured residuals, and supports control and protection decisions without bypassing hard safety limits. Physics-constrained estimation is preferable to an unconstrained black-box predictor whenever extrapolation could command unsafe converter states. The twin should include saturation, current limiting, bypass logic and protection events, because these govern performance during the disturbances that matter most.

### 9.9.5 Renewable energy, EV charging and storage integration

PV inverters, battery systems and EV chargers are both sources of power-quality stress and controllable converter resources. A UPQC sharing a DC link with PV or a battery can use spare converter headroom for reactive and harmonic compensation while also managing real power. The scheduling problem is multi-timescale: energy management acts over seconds to hours, harmonic and sag compensation over cycles or milliseconds. The converter current limit couples these services and requires explicit priority logic.

### 9.9.6 What constitutes research-level validation

A research contribution becomes more convincing as it moves up the validation ladder of FIG{ladder}. An analytical model should state network strength and $R/X$, load type, converter limits and sign conventions. EMT simulation should include switching or a justified averaged model, saturation, delay, DC-link dynamics and disturbance sequences. Sensitivity studies should vary grid impedance, load level and parameter error. Controller-HIL tests validate execution and timing; power-HIL adds power-interface dynamics; a prototype or field benchmark finally exposes sensing, dead time, thermal limits, protection and interference that ideal simulations hide. Comparisons should use identical hardware ratings and report the exact standard metric (for example TDD rather than THD), the detection delay and the transient peak current (TAB{validation}).

### 9.9.7 Industry perspective

Loads whose interruption is expensive, such as semiconductor fabrication, paper machines, plastics extrusion, data centres and hospitals, drive most custom-power installations. Field experience with inverter-based DVRs protecting industrial plants has been reported since the late 1990s [20], and medium-voltage DSTATCOMs of a few MVA are offered commercially, often coordinating switched capacitor and reactor banks. In each case the engineering argument rested on a measured disturbance profile compared with the equipment's own tolerance. A DVR or UPQC addresses sags; a UPS, standby generation or another topology is needed where outages dominate. The sub-millisecond responses simulated in this chapter do not by themselves prove that every protected process will be unaffected.

## Common Misconceptions

- *"A capacitor-supported DVR can correct any sag by injecting in quadrature."* For a standalone DVR the injection must be perpendicular to the load current, and the sag must satisfy $X\le1-\cos\phi$. A unity-pf load cannot be helped at all without real power.
- *"Quadrature to the supply voltage and quadrature to the current are the same thing."* They coincide only when the series current is in phase with the supply, as in a right-shunt UPQC with unity source pf.
- *"A zero-power series converter needs no DC energy."* It still has conduction, switching and magnetic losses.
- *"UPQC-Q can correct swells."* A perpendicular injection always increases the magnitude.
- *"The UPQC-P series rating is $V_{Lc}XI_L\cos\phi$."* The series converter carries the increased source current, so the factor $1/(1-X)$ is required.
- *"The smallest series VA means the smallest installation."* Check the shunt duty, the nominal-voltage duty and each converter's own maxima.
- *"A three-leg DSTATCOM can remove neutral current."* It needs a fourth leg, a split DC link or a zero-sequence transformer.
- *"A DSTATCOM's kVA rating includes the neutral-leg current times phase voltage."* The neutral leg's AC terminal is at neutral potential.
- *"A hybrid converter carries only residual source current."* In series with a passive branch it carries the branch current.
- *"A shunt filter is always the right answer for harmonics."* For voltage-source type loads a series impedance is more effective, and a passive shunt filter can resonate with the source.
- *"Passing current THD proves IEEE 519 compliance."* TDD, individual limits, voltage limits and the statistical tests are all required, and the low-voltage voltage-THD limit is 8 %, not 5 %.

## Chapter Summary

Custom power devices apply the converter principles of transmission FACTS to distribution power quality. Problems divide into voltage-quality problems, which a series device corrects, and current-quality problems, which a shunt device corrects.

The DSTATCOM is a shunt VSC with only a capacitor on its DC side. It supplies the reactive, unbalanced, zero-sequence and harmonic parts of the load current so that the source supplies only balanced active current, or it adds reactive current to regulate the PCC voltage. Its topologies range from the three-leg VSC for three-wire systems to four-leg, split-capacitor and transformer-assisted converters for four-wire systems. Its references come from unit-template, SRF, pq and related algorithms, and its design follows from the DC-bus voltage, capacitor energy, inductor ripple and switch-rating relations.

The DVR is a series VSC with an injection transformer, filter, bypass switch and a DC link supplied by storage, a rectifier or only a capacitor. In-phase injection minimizes voltage rating and draws $XP_L$ from storage; pre-sag injection preserves phase; phase-advance injection saves energy; quadrature injection needs no real power but is feasible for a standalone DVR only when $X\le1-\cos\phi$. Storage is sized from usable energy, real power and losses, and protection must bypass downstream faults without bypassing the upstream sags the DVR exists to correct.

The UPQC combines both on a common DC link, circulating the series converter's real power through the shunt converter. UPQC-P minimizes series voltage, UPQC-Q makes the series duty reactive but cannot correct swells, and UPQC-S chooses the angle to share power or minimize combined VA. Each converter's installed rating must cover its own worst condition, including nominal operation.

Harmonic filtering depends on the type of harmonic source and on the source impedance: shunt filters suit current-source loads, series filters suit voltage-source loads, and a series active filter with a passive shunt branch removes the dependence on source impedance at a small converter rating. Compliance with IEEE Std 519-2022 is judged by TDD, individual harmonics, voltage limits and statistical tests at the PCC. Device selection follows the measured disturbance profile, equipment tolerance, normalized converter ratings, storage requirements and a life-cycle cost assessment.

## Solved Numerical Practice Problems

The following problems are chapter-level synthesis exercises. Unless stated otherwise, three-phase quantities use 415 V line-to-line (239.6 V phase), and each solution connects the calculation with converter physics, rating limits and model assumptions.

#### EXDEF{ex_tx}. Injection transformer rating

A DVR for a 415 V, 20 kVA system must inject up to 171 V per phase on the line side, with a converter-side voltage of 50 V. Find the turns ratio, the transformer kVA and the converter-side current.

**Solution.** $n=50/171=0.292$. $I_s=27.82$ A, so $S=3\times171\times27.82=14.27$ kVA. The converter-side current is $27.82\times171/50=95.1$ A.

#### EXDEF{ex_zs_dvr}. DVR with source impedance: why zero-power injection fails

A 415 V supply with $Z_s=0.3+j1.8\ \Omega$ per phase feeds a star load $Z_L=18+j12\ \Omega$ per phase. The source EMF sags by 25 %. A DVR at the load must keep the load voltage at 239.6 V. (a) Find the load current and voltage without the DVR. (b) Can a capacitor-supported DVR restore the voltage? (c) Find the minimum-magnitude injection and its real power.

**Solution.** (a) $V_M=0.75\times239.6=179.7$ V, $I=179.7/|Z_s+Z_L|=7.84$ A and $|V_L|=7.84\times21.63=169.6$ V (293.8 V line). (b) With the load restored, $|I_L|=239.6/21.63=11.08$ A and the circuit resistance absorbs $11.08^2\times18.3=2245$ W per phase. The sagged EMF can deliver at most $179.7\times11.08=1990$ W at that current, so zero-real-power restoration is impossible. (c) $V_{inj}=V_L(1+Z_s/Z_L)-V_M$ is smallest when $V_L(1+Z_s/Z_L)$ is aligned with $V_M$: $|V_{inj}|=239.6\times1.0595-179.7=74.15$ V per phase. The DVR then supplies $P_{DVR}=1.97$ kW (three-phase) and its duty is 2.46 kVA. A quadrature injection of $\sqrt{239.6^2-179.7^2}=158.5$ V is not a valid zero-power answer here; the power-balance test in (b) settles feasibility before any injection angle is chosen.

#### EXDEF{ex_unbal}. Unbalanced sag compensation

At the DVR terminals $V_p=0.85\angle0^\circ$ pu, $V_n=0.15\angle0^\circ$ pu and $V_0=0$. Restore the balanced target $[1,a^2,a]$ pu.

**Solution.** From EQ{seq}, $V_a=1$, $V_b=-0.5-j0.6062=0.7858\angle-129.52^\circ$ and $V_c=0.7858\angle129.52^\circ$ pu. EQ{injk} gives $V_{inj,a}=0$, $V_{inj,b}=-j0.2598$ and $V_{inj,c}=+j0.2598$ pu. Phase a already meets its target; b and c need equal magnitudes with opposite angles. Restoring phase magnitudes alone does not ensure balanced phase relationships.

#### EXDEF{ex_angled}. Real and reactive power of an angled injection

A single-phase DVR injects 60 V at $45^\circ$ ahead of a 15 A load current. Find its P, Q and VA.

**Solution.** $P=60\times15\cos45^\circ=636.4$ W, $Q=636.4$ var and $S=900$ VA.

#### EXDEF{ex_idvr}. IDVR real-power sharing

Three series compensators share a DC bus. Feeder 2 needs 12 kW for 150 ms; feeders 1 and 3 can each absorb up to 8 kW while keeping acceptable voltage. Check sufficiency, neglecting losses.

**Solution.** 16 kW is available against 12 kW needed. Commanding 6 kW absorption on each supporting converter gives $P_1=-6$, $P_2=12$ and $P_3=-6$ kW, and the mean DC energy is constant. A standalone storage-only DVR would need $12\times0.15=1.8$ kJ. With total loss $P_{loss}$ the absorption must sum to $12+P_{loss}$ kW, so up to 4 kW of loss can be covered. This establishes steady power sufficiency, not zero DC capacitance: transient delay and ripple still need buffer energy.

#### EXDEF{ex_upqc_design}. Design of a 415 V, 30 kW three-phase four-wire UPQC

Design the series part of a UPQC for a 415 V, 30 kW load with $\pm30$ % supply variation, a 700 V DC bus, $f_s=10$ kHz, $m=1$, $a=1.2$ and 10 % current ripple. Size the DC-link capacitor for a 5 % droop over 10 ms at the shunt converter's overload current.

**Solution.** $V_{DVR}=0.3\times239.6=71.9$ V and $K_{DVR}=239.6/71.9=3.33$; a ratio of 3 is chosen. During the deepest sag $I_s=30\,000/(3\times0.7\times239.6)=59.6$ A, so the transformer is rated $3\times71.9\times59.6=12.86$ kVA and the series VSC device current is $59.6/3=19.9$ A (32 A devices with margin). During the largest swell $I_s=30\,000/(3\times1.3\times239.6)=32.1$ A, and EQ{ldvr} with $\Delta I=3.21$ A gives $L_{DVR}=0.866\times700\times3/(6\times1.2\times10^4\times3.21)=7.87$ mH. The ripple filter uses $R_r=5\ \Omega$ and $C_r=10\ \mu$F. The shunt converter is designed as in Section 9.3.4 at $V_{dc}=677.7\to700$ V. With $I=69.56$ A and $a=1.2$, EQ{cdc} gives $\Delta E=3\times239.6\times1.2\times69.56\times0.01=600$ J and $C_{dc}=2\times600/(700^2-665^2)=25.1$ mF. The four-leg shunt converter does not pass neutral current through the capacitor, so EQ{csplit} does not apply.

#### EXDEF{ex_hyb_branch}. Hybrid branch current and residual source harmonic

For the topology of FIG{apf}(b), a load draws 40 A RMS of fifth harmonic. Assume, for this simplified same-phase exercise, that the passive branch diverts 92 % under nominal tuning and 85 % when detuned. Find the residual source harmonic and the fifth-harmonic current through the active stage. Is a 10 A stage enough?

**Solution.** Nominal: $I_{s,5}=40\times0.08=3.2$ A and the branch carries 36.8 A. Detuned: 6.0 A residual and 34.0 A in the branch. The active stage in series with the branch carries 36.8 A or 34.0 A, so a 10 A stage is insufficient; a 45 A stage gives 22 % margin over the nominal case, before the branch's fundamental capacitive current is added (EQ{saf}). Assigning the 6 A residual to this converter would confuse it with a separate shunt residual-current stage. In a real network the current division is complex-valued and depends on the source and filter impedances (EQ{passive}).

#### EXDEF{ex_tdd}. PCC TDD and individual-harmonic screening

An installation at a 120 V to 69 kV PCC has $I_L=400$ A and $I_{sc}=14$ kA. Its RMS spectrum is $I_5=14$, $I_7=9$, $I_{11}=5$, $I_{13}=3.5$ and $I_{17}=2$ A, with $I_1=380$ A. Screen it.

**Solution.** $I_{sc}/I_L=35$, the 20 to 50 band: TDD limit 8 %, individual limits 7 % (5th, 7th), 3.5 % (11th, 13th) and 2.5 % (17th). $I_H=\sqrt{318.25}=17.84$ A, so TDD = 4.46 % and THD = 4.69 %. The individual values are 3.5, 2.25, 1.25, 0.875 and 0.5 %, all within limits. A shunt APF that reduced every harmonic by 80 % would bring TDD to 0.89 %, but the spectrum already passes this screen, so such a filter would need a separate justification. Full compliance also needs the statistical assessment of Section 9.7.4.
